## Facts used in the script (all checked)

| Line | Source |
|---|---|
| One foot of rushing water can carry your car away | NWS Turn Around Don't Drown: "It takes just 12 inches of rushing water to carry away most cars" |
| Even if the car in front made it | NSSL: people drive in "because the vehicle in front of them makes it" |
| Flash floods rise fast | NWS Melbourne: urban and small-stream flash floods "often occur in less than one hour" |
| You can't tell how deep it is | NWS Burlington: "It is impossible to tell the exact depth of water covering a roadway" |
| Six inches reaches the bottom of your car; the engine can stall | NWS Tulsa: "Six inches of water will reach the bottom of most passenger cars causing loss of control and possible stalling" |
| At one foot your car can start to float | NWS Tulsa: "A foot of water will float many vehicles" |
| A car is basically a box of air (buoyancy) | NWS buoyancy sheet: vehicles, like ships, float because of buoyancy |
| About 1,500 pounds of lift per foot of water | NWS Burlington: "A foot of water will impart 1500 pounds of lift on an average sized vehicle" |
| The current pushes with hundreds of pounds of force | NWS Burlington: water at 6 to 12 mph pushes "with 500 to 700 pounds of force for each foot the water rises" |
| Two feet carries away SUVs and trucks | NWS: "just 2 feet of rushing water can carry away SUVs and trucks" |
| The road might not be there | NWS: "The road may have collapsed under that water" |
| More than half of flood drownings happen in vehicles | NWS citing the CDC: "over half of all flood-related drownings occur when a vehicle is driven into hazardous flood water" |
| Get out and get to higher ground, if it's safe | NWS Tulsa: "If floodwaters rise around your car, abandon the car and move to higher ground if you can do so safely" |
