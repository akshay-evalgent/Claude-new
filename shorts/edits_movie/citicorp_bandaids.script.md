# The skyscraper that could have fallen: Citicorp Center, 1978 (true story, draft script)

**Tone:** suspense, with an engineer who owned up to his own mistake.
- **Left out:** LeMessurier's passing thought of suicide, because of ad safety.
- **The student:** the student who called is told plainly as "a student's question". Diane Hartley's role is disputed, so it goes in the description only.
- **The risk:** framed as "his calculations showed", because a 2019 NIST study questioned the risk.

## Script (about 100 s; REAL marks LeMessurier on camera, from his 1995 MIT lecture)

1. **Hook:** In 1978, a brand-new New York skyscraper had a secret. A big storm could knock it over. And only one man knew.
2. This is Citicorp Center: fifty-nine floors, finished in 1977. Look at the bottom. It stands on four giant stilts, and they're not on the corners. They're in the middle of each side.
3. That's because a church owned the corner of the lot, so the tower was built right over it.
4. Its engineer, William LeMessurier, was proud of it. Then, in 1978, a student's question made him recheck his math, for winds hitting the building at an angle.
5. He found those winds put up to forty percent more strain on some of its braces.
6. Then he found something worse. To save money, the builder had swapped welded joints for bolts. His own office had approved it, without him knowing.
7. Together, that meant up to a hundred and sixty percent more force on those bolts.
8. His calculations showed a storm strong enough to break them hits New York about once every sixteen years.
9. **REAL:** "...the return period to failure was sixteen years. Think about that."
10. **REAL:** "I am the only person in the world who knew this."
11. He could have stayed quiet, and nobody would ever have known. Instead, he told the bank.
12. So every night, after the office workers went home, welders covered more than two hundred joints with two-inch-thick steel plates.
13. **REAL:** "...like Band-Aids. Literally, Band-Aids."
14. Every morning, they cleaned up before anyone arrived. Quietly, police and the Red Cross planned to evacuate ten blocks around it.
15. Then, on September first, Hurricane Ella was heading north, toward New York.
16. And then it turned out to sea.
17. The newspapers never got the story, because every major paper in New York had just gone on strike.
18. **REAL:** "...that was the greatest thing that ever happened."
19. The public found out seventeen years later, in 1995.
20. **Doubt:** A 2019 government study suggests the diagonal winds may not have been as dangerous as he calculated. But he didn't wait to find out.
21. **REAL (ending):** "This was my building... I have to save it." (or "this thing could kill thousands, you must do something")
22. **Loop:** So next time you see this skyscraper standing on stilts... it's held together with Band-Aids.
