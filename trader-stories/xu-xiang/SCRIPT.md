# Xu Xiang: script, draft 1

Working title: **The Man Who Always Knew First**

About 2220 narrated words. At 170 words a minute with a quarter of the runtime left for picture and music, that is about 18 minutes. Narration is the plain paragraphs. Lines
marked PICTURE describe what is on screen. SCORE is the on-screen readout.
Every fact is in `RESEARCH.md`; anything marked Legend there is told as
rumour here.

---

## 1. Cold open (0:00-1:00, 123 words)

PICTURE: Black. A clock readout: 10:33. A phone screen, a short police notice in Chinese with a translation under it.

Sunday. The first of November, 2015. Ten thirty-three in the morning.

In the city of Ningbo, the highway police post a short notice online. Sudden traffic control. The entrances to the Hangzhou Bay Bridge are closed.

PICTURE: The bridge from high above, a thin line over grey water running to the horizon. A distance counter: 35.4 km.

The bridge is thirty-five kilometres long. It crosses open sea.

PICTURE: One car on an empty expressway, seen from behind.

And on the road towards it, there is one car that does not know.

The man inside left his grandmother's hundredth birthday party a few minutes ago. He left in a hurry. He is wearing a white coat.

PICTURE: A falling market chart, red. Then one green line climbing through it.

He controls more money than any hedge fund manager in China. This year, the market lost five trillion dollars. His best fund is up three hundred and fifty-seven percent.

For twenty-two years, he has known everything first.

Not today.

PICTURE: Title card.

## 2. The legend (1:00-2:00, 127 words)

SCORE: 2015 / 28,000,000,000 yuan under his control

PICTURE: A trading floor seen from the front. At the very back, one figure in a tracksuit.

His name is Xu Xiang. You have probably never heard it. In China, they called him Hedge Fund Brother Number One.

He gave almost no interviews. He came to work in a tracksuit. He sat at the back of his own trading floor.

PICTURE: Two phones on a table, one showing prices, one showing headlines.

In meetings he watched two phones. One for prices. One for news. He let everyone else talk.

PICTURE: Quote card, credited to "a longtime friend, to the New York Times Magazine".

A friend put it like this. "Xu Xiang is always trading. If he's not trading, he's thinking about trading."

PICTURE: Two bars growing side by side. One stops almost at once. The other leaves the frame.

His first fund went up three thousand two hundred and seventy percent in five and a half years. Over the same years, the Shanghai index rose eleven.

Nobody could explain it. Plenty of people had a theory.

And in the summer of 2015, somebody decided to find out.

## 3. Origin (2:00-3:10, 149 words)

SCORE: 1990 / 0 yuan

PICTURE: Shanghai, 1990. The exchange opening, in the house style. Then a schoolboy at a desk with a newspaper folded to the share prices.

Go back to 1990.

Shanghai opens a stock exchange. A whole country starts learning what a share is, all at the same time.

Xu Xiang is in his first year of high school, in Ningbo, a port city down the coast. His father works in a factory. His mother keeps the house. Nobody in the family has ever owned a share.

He starts trading anyway, while he is still at school.

PICTURE: Stacks of books. A lecture hall seen from the back row.

There is no teacher. He reads books. He sits in on lectures. He copies whatever he can find about how foreigners do it.

PICTURE: An exam hall, rows of bent heads, one empty desk.

Then comes the university entrance exam. In China, that exam decides your life.

He doesn't take it.

PICTURE: A hand passing over a bundle of notes. Counter: 30,000 yuan.

Instead, he asks his parents for thirty thousand yuan. About five thousand dollars. And he carries it to a trading hall on South Liberation Road.

SCORE: 1993 / 30,000 yuan

Years later, he would say that 1993 was the year he was born.

## 4. The hall (3:10-4:30, 176 words)

PICTURE: A concrete staircase to a second floor. A hall packed with people facing a blackboard covered in chalk numbers.

The hall belongs to Galaxy Securities. Second floor, up a concrete staircase. The prices are written on blackboards, in chalk. The rules change so often that nobody is sure what they are this month.

PICTURE: Chart drawn in chalk. It climbs steeply, then drops off the board.

And he has picked a terrible moment. In 1992, the Shanghai index rose a hundred and sixty-seven percent. Between April 1993 and the summer of 1994, it falls by three quarters.

PICTURE: The crowd in the hall thins, frame by frame. A bunch of green chives on a chopping board.

Most of the people in that hall are finished. In China there is a word for them. Chives. You cut them. They grow back. You cut them again.

Xu Xiang does not get cut.

PICTURE: Three words, one at a time.

He builds a style, and he never changes it. Buy fast. Sell fast. Always go big.

PICTURE: Tag on screen: RUMOUR. Two silhouettes facing each other across a table.

By 1995 he is nineteen, and people in Ningbo are telling a story about him. Two Shanghai gangsters, they say, fell out over which of them would get him as a personal trader. A triad boss had to settle it.

Nobody has ever proved that story. But people believed it. At nineteen, he was already someone you could believe that about.

## 5. The method (4:30-7:00, 314 words)

PICTURE: A price line climbing. It hits a flat ceiling marked +10 % and stops.

Then the market did something that made his whole career possible.

In December 1996, Chinese shares get a ceiling. A stock can rise ten percent in a day. Then it stops. That is the limit.

The rule is there to calm things down. Now think about what it really does.

PICTURE: An order book. One side is a tall stack of buy orders. The other side is empty. A queue of small figures forms behind the stack.

A stock that hits the ceiling still has buyers. They wanted in. They couldn't get in. So tomorrow morning, they try again.

The limit does not stop a rush. It makes the rush wait in line.

PICTURE: Five figures at one desk in the chalk hall. The scoreboard pulses.

Xu and a handful of traders at Galaxy work out how to start that line.

PICTURE: A diagram in four steps, each drawn as he says it.

Pick a cheap stock that nobody is watching. Hit it with one enormous buy order, out of nowhere. Other traders see the size and pile in. Limit up.

Next morning, the buyers who missed out push it to the limit again.

A few days later, sell everything to the people still arriving.

Then the price falls. But you are already gone.

PICTURE: A newspaper headline in Chinese, translated: LIMIT-UP DEATH SQUAD.

The press gives them a name. The Limit-Up Death Squad. Xu Xiang, in his early twenties, is the captain.

PICTURE: A map of China. Dots light up in city after city, all pointing at Ningbo.

Soon they barely need the trick. Traders across the country watch which stocks the Ningbo hall is buying, and buy the same ones. Their order has become the news.

PICTURE: A row of sports cars parked outside the grey trading hall. One empty space.

The others buy sports cars and park them outside the hall. Xu doesn't. He buys more stock.

There are books about them. Travelling seminars. Copycat squads in other cities.

PICTURE: A newspaper front page, dated 2003. A man in a suit arriving with a briefcase.

And in 2003, a newspaper asks the obvious question. Is any of this allowed?

The regulator sends an investigator to Ningbo. He calls in the big local traders for what is described as an informal discussion.

PICTURE: The hall, empty. A calendar flips seven pages. The hall, full again.

The Death Squad vanishes. For one week.

Then the investigator announces that he has found no wrongdoing. And they come back.

Remember that week. It matters later.

SCORE: 2005 / hundreds of millions of yuan

By 2005, the boy with thirty thousand yuan has hundreds of millions.

## 6. Zexi (7:00-9:50, 362 words)

PICTURE: The Shanghai skyline. A glass office, lights on.

Ningbo is now too small. He moves to Shanghai, and his timing is perfect. That same year, a change in the law lets private funds step out of the shadows and operate in the open. They call them sunshine funds.

PICTURE: A company registration document. Two characters, each with a portrait beside it.

In December 2009, he registers his own firm. He calls it Zexi. Ze, from Mao Zedong. Xi, from Kangxi, the longest-reigning emperor in Chinese history.

Modest, he was not.

SCORE: 2010 / 1,000,000,000 yuan in the first fund

PICTURE: A list of investor names, every line blacked out. Counter: fewer than 200.

In March 2010 he opens his first fund with one billion yuan. It has fewer than two hundred investors. Under the rules, that means he never has to say who they are.

Remember that, too.

PICTURE: A wall clock at 8:45. The office empties around the tracksuit figure. The clock reads 2:00.

He is at his desk at eight forty-five every morning. He is often still there at two the next morning. His own researchers only find out whether he took their advice at the end of the year, when the results come in.

Friends say he has no hobbies. There is the market. That is all there is.

PICTURE: A diagram: a company, a board table, a chair being filled, coins flowing back.

The method has grown up with him. He no longer just hits a stock and runs. He buys a real stake in a small company. He gets allies onto the board. The company pays out cash. He uses the cash to buy more, and sells into the rise.

Between 2010 and 2014 he does this with forty-five companies. His own parents sit as executives and shareholders across the web of firms.

PICTURE: The two bars from the opening, now with numbers. A rich list scrolling to number 188.

And the results are absurd. One fund returns five hundred and thirty-six percent in four years. By 2015 he is the hundred and eighty-eighth richest person in China, worth two point two billion dollars.

PICTURE: Tag on screen: UNPROVEN. The blacked-out list again.

So who were the two hundred? It was said, and never proven, that some of them were the children of very senior officials. A man who had worked in the industry told a reporter that when a powerful client hands you money, it is difficult to say no.

PICTURE: A map of central Beijing. Two pins, very close together. A distance line: under half a mile.

In November 2014, Xu opens an office in Beijing. It is less than half a mile from the headquarters of the stock market regulator.

His colleagues later said that was the start of a year spent trying to protect himself. He could feel something coming.

He was right.

## 7. The crash (9:50-12:25, 337 words)

PICTURE: The same ceiling shot as section 5, upside down. A line falls and hits a floor marked -10 %.

Then the market did something that made his whole career impossible.

PICTURE: Date card. Then the Shanghai index, drawn day by day, going over the edge.

Friday, the twelfth of June, 2015. The Chinese market peaks. Then it falls.

In three weeks, a third of its value is gone.

PICTURE: A sea of small figures, each holding a phone. Counter: 200,000,000.

Remember who is in this market. Two hundred million ordinary people. They make about eighty-five percent of the trades. Chives, as far as you can see.

PICTURE: Three stamps hitting a page: SELLING BANNED. LISTINGS HALTED. BUY.

The government throws everything at it. Big shareholders are banned from selling. New listings are frozen. State firms are ordered to buy. They call it the national team, and it has up to three trillion yuan behind it.

It does not hold.

PICTURE: The chart again. Two more cliffs, each dated.

The twenty-seventh of July. Another plunge.

The twenty-fourth of August. Shanghai falls eight and a half percent, its worst day in eight years. In New York, the Dow drops a thousand points at the open. The state newspaper calls it Black Monday.

SCORE: Summer 2015 / market: -5,000,000,000,000 dollars

Five trillion dollars, gone in ten weeks.

PICTURE: Five green lines rising through the red. A league table of 1,649 names; one moves to the top.

And Zexi?

In the worst three weeks of the crash, all five of his public funds go up. Every one, by at least twenty percent. His best fund is up three hundred and fifty-seven percent for the year. First place, out of one thousand six hundred and forty-nine.

His money seemed to step out of the market just before each fall.

In a normal year, that makes you a genius.

In a year when the state itself lost, what does it make you?

PICTURE: Beijing at night. A long corridor of closed office doors.

Meanwhile, in Beijing, people need an answer. The Party's own inspectors have begun going through the stock regulator from top to bottom. Brokerage executives are being arrested. Everyone in finance is asking the same thing. Who is next?

PICTURE: Tag on screen: ALLEGATION. A social media post spreading from phone to phone.

In September, an anonymous post goes viral. It claims that during the rescue, Zexi worked with a state-owned brokerage to point the government's own buying at a stock Zexi wanted lifted.

Zexi denied it. No court ever ruled on it.

PICTURE: A passport. A red stamp across it.

But the regulator is now looking. And Xu Xiang is told he may not leave the country.

Nine days later, his grandmother turns one hundred.

## 8. The bridge (12:25-14:05, 164 words)

No narration for the first 20 seconds. Music only.

PICTURE: A plain apartment in an old neighbourhood. A long table, a cake, a very old woman at the head of it. The white coat on the back of a chair.

The party is small. His grandmother still lives in the neighbourhood where he grew up.

PICTURE: A phone face down on the table. It lights up. Clock: 10:28.

Shortly before ten thirty, according to people who knew him, a warning reaches him. They are coming.

He leaves at once.

PICTURE: The car, from above, through grey apartment blocks. A river. Then the bay opening out on the left.

He drives north, towards Shanghai. Past the concrete blocks. Over the river that cuts Ningbo in half. Out along the bay.

PICTURE: A road map reduced to one line and one bridge.

The fast road to Shanghai has one bridge.

PICTURE: Clock: 10:33. The police notice from the cold open.

For twenty-two years, his whole edge had been knowing before everyone else. This morning he knew, too. A few minutes late. And not about the bridge.

PICTURE: Brake lights. Blue lights. Then black.

They stop him there.

Hold on black for 4 seconds.

PICTURE: The photograph, redrawn in the house style: white coat, rimless glasses, a hand pulling back his sleeve, a handcuff. He looks straight at us.

That evening, a photograph appears online. White coat. Rimless glasses. Someone's hand is pulling back his sleeve, and you can see the cuff. He is looking straight into the camera.

PICTURE: Comment bubbles stacking up over the photo.

The internet does what it does. The coat, people said, looked like a doctor's. Had he been arrested for practising medicine without a licence?

Two hundred million people had just been cut. Now they had someone to look at.

## 9. The answer (14:05-15:40, 206 words)

PICTURE: Office doors with paper seals across them. Empty desks.

Then, nothing. When reporters reach Zexi's offices in Shanghai and Beijing, the doors are already sealed. There is a word for what has happened to him. Shilian. Lost contact.

The firm does not survive it. As one profile put it: Xu was Zexi.

PICTURE: A courthouse. Date card: 5 December 2016, Qingdao.

Thirteen months later, he appears in a courtroom in Qingdao. And he pleads guilty.

Now we get the answer. How did he always know?

PICTURE: The four-step diagram from section 5, redrawn larger, with a boardroom added at the start.

From 2010 to 2015, the court found, Xu worked with executives at thirteen listed companies. The company would put out good news. Xu would buy heavily, and the price would climb. Ordinary investors would see it and rush in. Then Xu and the executives would sell to them.

He knew first because he had arranged what there was to know.

PICTURE: Hold the old Death Squad diagram and the new one side by side.

Look at it again. It is the same trick as the chalk hall. Start the line. Sell to the people at the back of it. The only thing that changed in twenty years was the size.

SCORE: 23 January 2017 / fine: 11,000,000,000 yuan

PICTURE: A gavel. The figures land one at a time.

On the twenty-third of January 2017, the sentence. Five and a half years. And a fine reported at eleven billion yuan. One point six billion dollars. The largest ever handed to one person for a financial crime in China.

He does not appeal.

---

## 10. The reckoning (15:40-16:50, 151 words)

PICTURE: The empty Ningbo hall for one week, from section 5. Then the sealed Zexi doors.

So here is the question this story leaves you with.

In 2003, an investigator looked at the Death Squad and found nothing wrong. In 2017, a court looked at the grown-up version and sent its captain to prison.

Was he a criminal who finally got caught? Or one player in a game that everybody was playing, picked out because the country needed a face?

PICTURE: Attribution line: "a fund manager, to the New York Times Magazine".

One fund manager said that many others could have been detained for exactly the same reasons.

The court's answer is simpler. He broke the law. He said so himself.

Both of those can be true.

PICTURE: A legal filing. A family photo frame, turned face down.

It cost him more than money. In March 2019, with Xu still in prison, his wife filed for divorce. They had met in 1998, when he was still the kid from the chalk hall. Around twenty billion yuan of assets sat frozen. And the industry he had ruled banned him from it.

---

## 11. The theme (16:50-17:10, 42 words)

PICTURE: The two phones from section 2, both screens dark.

His gift was never picking stocks.

It was knowing first. He built a life on it, and for twenty-two years it worked.

But a man who lives by knowing first has exactly one weakness. The day somebody else knows before he does.

---

## 12. Epilogue (17:10-17:45, 69 words)

SCORE: 9 July 2021 / fine reported unpaid

PICTURE: A prison gate. A figure walking out, small in the frame. No white coat.

On the ninth of July 2021, Xu Xiang walked out of prison. He was forty-four. The fine, by the last reports, had still not been paid.

PICTURE: Quote card, credited to "a Beijing fund manager, to the New York Times Magazine".

A fund manager in Beijing once summed up the whole business in one sentence. "In China, the losing side doesn't get to sleep at night."

PICTURE: The bridge from above, as in the cold open. Traffic starts to move.

As for the bridge. It reopened after a little more than half an hour.

The traffic moved on.

PICTURE: Sources card. Then end.
