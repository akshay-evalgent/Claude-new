# Liu Qibing: script, draft 1

Working title: **The Trader Who Didn't Exist**

Narration is the plain paragraphs. PICTURE lines describe the screen. Facts and
their status are in `RESEARCH.md`.

---

## 1. Cold open

PICTURE: Black. Date card: LONDON · NOVEMBER 2005. A wall of glowing price screens, one number climbing.

London. November 2005. The price of copper is at a record high, and it keeps climbing.

PICTURE: Traders on phones in a dim trading room.

On the trading floors of the London Metal Exchange, a rumour is spreading. Somewhere in this market, a trader working for the Chinese government has bet that copper will fall. A huge bet. Well over a hundred thousand tonnes of copper he does not own, promised for delivery before Christmas.

PICTURE: An empty desk. A chair pushed back. A single folded note.

And the trader has vanished.

PICTURE: A government building in Beijing at night.

When reporters ask the Chinese government about him, the first answer is that it does not know who he is.

PICTURE: Title card.

---

## 2. The man nobody knew

PICTURE: A slim man in a white shirt at a desk of screens, seen from behind.

His name was Liu Qibing. Inside China's copper world, he was not unknown at all. He was the star trader of the State Reserve Bureau, the agency that keeps the country's emergency stockpiles of metal.

PICTURE: Quote card: CHINA'S LEESON.

Chinese newspapers would later call him China's Leeson, after the trader who brought down Barings Bank.

PICTURE: A ledger page, a column of numbers in red.

His losses on paper would reach six hundred and six million dollars.

So how does a state employee end up betting against the entire market? And when the bet goes wrong, who pays?

---

## 3. The wire to London

PICTURE: A university campus in Wuhan in the early 1990s, students with bicycles under plane trees.

Liu studied economics at Wuhan University, then joined the trading arm of the State Reserve Bureau.

PICTURE: Rows of copper cathodes stacked in a vast grey warehouse.

The bureau's job was simple. Keep stockpiles of strategic metals like copper, aluminium and zinc. Buy when prices are low. Release metal when prices spike. Keep the country supplied.

PICTURE: A young man at a computer terminal in a London office in 1995, cables everywhere.

In 1995, the bureau sent Liu to London for six months, to the London Metal Exchange. He helped build the computer link between the exchange and his bureau in Beijing.

He was, in a very literal sense, the bureau's wire to the world's metal market.

PICTURE: The London Metal Exchange ring: traders on red benches in a circle, shouting.

The LME is where the world sets the price of copper. Miners, smelters, factories and speculators meet there. And in 1998, Liu's centre began trading on it with its own money. Liu placed the trades.

---

## 4. The boom

SCORE: 2003 · COPPER ~$1,600 / tonne

PICTURE: Beijing skyline crowded with construction cranes.

Then China began to build. Roads, towers, power lines, whole new cities. Every one of them needed copper.

PICTURE: Chart: copper climbing from about $1,600 in 2003 to $3,000 by late 2004.

In 2003, copper was trading at around sixteen hundred dollars a tonne. By the end of 2004, it was close to three thousand.

Liu had bet on the rise. According to a later academic study, his trades earned the state around three hundred million dollars. He became the centre's chief trader.

PICTURE: Two exchange boards side by side: SHANGHAI and LONDON.

He also ran a quieter trade. Copper traded in two places, Shanghai and London, and the two prices did not always agree. Foreign investors could not trade in Shanghai. Liu could trade in both. So from around 2003, by that academic account, he would typically buy in Shanghai and sell in London, and pocket the gap.

It meant that selling copper in London was nothing new to him. It was part of his routine.

PICTURE: An office door at night, one desk lit inside.

And here is the detail that matters. By the middle of 2004, he was the only authorised trader in his group. For more than a year, nobody else in the room traded what he traded.

---

## 5. The turn

PICTURE: Close-up: a man's reflection in a screen, the chart behind him near its peak.

As copper neared three thousand dollars, Liu came to a conclusion. The boom had gone too far. Prices had run ahead of reality, and they would fall.

So he turned around. He started betting that copper would drop.

PICTURE: A meeting room, colleagues across a table, one shaking his head.

According to the Economic Observer, people advised him to stop. He did not.

PICTURE: Diagram: SELLING CALL OPTIONS.

In October 2004, copper fell ten percent in a single day. To Liu, it looked like the turn had arrived. He went further, selling large numbers of call options, beyond what he was authorised to do.

Here is what that means. Selling a call option is collecting a small fee now, for promising to sell someone copper later at a fixed price. If prices stay put, you keep the fee. If prices soar, you still have to sell at the old price. There is no ceiling on how much you can lose.

---

## 6. The promise

SCORE: 2005 · SHORT ~130,000-220,000 TONNES

PICTURE: Diagram: a short sale on the exchange. Promise now, deliver on a date.

On the LME, a bet that copper will fall works like a promise. You agree today to deliver copper on a future date, at today's price. If the price falls before then, you buy cheap and deliver at the higher price. If it rises, you have to buy expensive copper to keep your promise, or pay to get out.

PICTURE: Calendar page: 21 DECEMBER 2005.

Through 2005, Liu's promise grew. By the summer, reports put it at around a hundred and thirty thousand tonnes, most of it due on the twenty-first of December. By some reports it later reached more than two hundred thousand.

PICTURE: An LME warehouse, half empty, a forklift moving a single stack.

To put that in perspective, all the copper sitting in the exchange's warehouses at the time was about a hundred and forty thousand tonnes.

He had promised to deliver more copper than the whole market had in stock.

---

## 7. The hunt

SCORE: SEPTEMBER 2005 · COPPER ~$3,800

PICTURE: Traders huddled round a screen, one pointing.

In a market like the LME, a position that big is hard to hide. Traders started to notice the selling. And then they started asking a question.

Does the bureau actually have the copper to deliver?

PICTURE: Chart: copper climbing through 2005 from $3,400 to $4,000.

If it didn't, every trader who bought against Liu would win. Because when December came, he would have to buy copper back from them, at whatever price they asked.

So, reportedly, they bought. And copper kept climbing. Three thousand four hundred in January. Three thousand eight hundred in September. Four thousand in October.

PICTURE: A man alone at a desk late at night, a phone face down beside him.

Each step up cost Liu tens of millions of dollars. Reports later said his supervisors pressed him to double up rather than admit the losses.

PICTURE: Number card: −$606,000,000.

By October, his loss on paper had reached six hundred and six million dollars.

PICTURE: A folded handwritten note on an empty desk, a pen beside it.

And in October 2005, Liu Qibing left a farewell note, and disappeared.

---

## 8. The disappearance

PICTURE: Misty mountains in Yunnan, a winding road.

He did not stay gone for long. In October, he was detained in Yunnan, in China's far south-west.

PICTURE: A London trading floor, screens glowing, phones ringing.

But the market did not know that. For weeks, the position just sat there. Unmanaged. With the delivery date coming closer every day.

PICTURE: Newspapers stacked at a London news stand.

In the middle of November, the story broke in the international press. A Chinese state trader was missing, and his short position was enormous.

---

## 9. The denial

SCORE: NOVEMBER 2005 · COPPER RECORD $4,243

PICTURE: A government spokesman's podium, empty, flags behind it.

Then came the strangest part of the story.

At first, the State Reserve Bureau said it did not know him. Then it said he was on leave. Then it said he had acted alone, and that any losses were his, not the state's.

PICTURE: Number card: 1,300,000 TONNES vs ~250,000.

The bureau said it held one point three million tonnes of copper in reserve. Market analysts thought the real figure was nearer two hundred and fifty thousand.

PICTURE: An auction room, a gavel, buyers with paddles.

It began auctioning copper from the stockpile, twenty thousand tonnes at a time. When one report said the bureau would export two hundred thousand tonnes, copper fell ninety dollars in five minutes.

PICTURE: Chart: copper hitting a record.

It did not hold. Copper hit a record of four thousand two hundred and forty-three dollars.

PICTURE: Quote card: THE GREATEST BLUFF OF ALL TIME?

Some in London had a darker theory. An analyst at a French bank suggested the whole thing might be the greatest bluff of all time. Perhaps the short position was a smoke screen. Perhaps China wanted prices pushed up, so it could sell its stockpile at the top. He admitted he might be wrong. But the theory showed how little anyone trusted what they were being told.

PICTURE: Two letterheads: a UK regulator and the exchange.

There was a bigger worry too. Britain's financial regulator warned the exchange that if Liu had traded without permission, the bureau might not have to honour his trades at all. The exchange answered that if it didn't, the brokers who had cleared those trades would be on the hook.

In other words, a missing man in China might leave London's brokers holding the bill.

PICTURE: Quote card: Jeremy Goldwyn, Sucden.

As one London broker told the New York Times, most of us don't know the full details, or the size of any Chinese positions, or if the positions exist at all.

---

## 10. The clean-up

SCORE: 21 DECEMBER 2005 · DELIVERY DAY

PICTURE: Cargo ships at a Korean port, cranes unloading copper bundles.

In the end, the bureau did the only thing it could. It found the metal.

Around the delivery date, about fifty thousand tonnes of copper turned up in LME warehouses in South Korea, most of it shipped from China. The rest of the position was rolled forward into 2006 and 2007, and closed piece by piece.

PICTURE: Chart: copper climbing past $8,000 in May 2006.

And copper kept going up. By May 2006, it was close to nine thousand dollars a tonne.

PICTURE: Number card: $606M → $144M.

Even so, with physical delivery, auctions and time, the bureau cut the loss from more than six hundred million dollars to a hundred and forty-four million. Counting an earlier profit at home, the final bill came to nine hundred and twenty million yuan.

---

## 11. The verdict

SCORE: 2008 · 7 YEARS

PICTURE: A Beijing courthouse in winter.

In 2008, Liu Qibing went on trial in Beijing. The court found that he had used state money for speculative trading abroad that was not hedging, against the rules for state-owned companies.

PICTURE: A courtroom seen from the gallery, a defendant standing alone.

He was sentenced to seven years in prison for abuse of power.

His direct boss got six years. The court found he had approved the trades, knowing the rules, and hidden them when he left his post.

PICTURE: Number card: 1997 · ZINC · 16 YEARS.

There was a precedent. In 1997, staff at a state smelter in Zhuzhou had also bet on falling metal prices in London, shorting zinc. They lost close to one and a half billion yuan. The official responsible got sixteen years. By that measure, an expert told the Economic Observer, seven years was not especially severe.

PICTURE: Two stamps on a page: SCAPEGOAT? ROGUE?

Some Chinese commentators said Liu was a scapegoat for a system that had let one man trade alone for a year. Others said he was a rogue who gambled with the nation's money. The bureau's trading arm was barred from speculating again.

---

## 12. Too early

SCORE: DECEMBER 2008 · COPPER ~$2,750

PICTURE: Chart: the full copper line from 2003 to 2008, his average short price marked, the crash at the end.

And here is the final twist.

Liu Qibing believed copper was in a bubble. By one academic account, he had sold at an average of around three and a half thousand dollars a tonne.

In late 2008, as the global financial crisis hit, copper crashed. By Christmas, it was trading at around two thousand seven hundred and fifty dollars. Below the price he had sold at.

He was right about copper. He was just three years early.

PICTURE: An empty trading desk, screens off.

In markets, being early and being wrong look exactly the same, right up until the day you run out of time.

Liu Qibing ran out of time in October 2005.

PICTURE: Sources card. Then end.
