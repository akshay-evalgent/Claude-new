# Juan Pablo Dávila: script, draft 1

Working title: **The Wrong Key**

Narration is the plain paragraphs. PICTURE lines describe the screen. Facts and
their status are in `RESEARCH.md`.

---

## 1. Cold open

PICTURE: Black. Date card: SANTIAGO · FRIDAY 21 JANUARY 1994. A car on a coastal road, the sea behind it.

Friday, the twenty-first of January, 1994. A man cuts his beach holiday short and heads back to Santiago.

PICTURE: A corridor in a 1990s office building. A man in a suit walking fast.

He asks to see his boss. He says it's urgent.

PICTURE: Graphic: a trade ticket. SELL flips to BUY.

That afternoon, he explains. A few months ago, he says, he made a mistake on his computer. He entered a sale as a purchase. Buy instead of sell.

The mistake cost thirty million dollars.

PICTURE: An office at night, one man sitting alone across a desk from another.

And instead of reporting it, he tried to win it back.

PICTURE: Title card.

---

## 2. The copper company

PICTURE: Aerial: the vast open-pit copper mine at Chuquicamata, terraces spiralling down.

The money belonged to Codelco, Chile's state copper company. The biggest copper company in the world.

PICTURE: Number card: 10% OF THE WORLD'S COPPER.

Codelco mined about a tenth of all the world's copper. Its sales brought in about a fifth of Chile's export earnings.

PICTURE: A copper ingot stamped with a star, on a dark table.

Chile had nationalised its copper mines in 1971. President Salvador Allende called copper "the salary of Chile". Codelco was not just a company. It was the country's wallet.

PICTURE: A young man in a white shirt and tie at a desk with a phone, early 1990s.

The man who lost the money was its head of futures trading. Juan Pablo Dávila. He was thirty-four.

He was an accountant. He had joined Codelco in 1986 as a trainee, on a salary of eight thousand six hundred and eighty dollars a year.

PICTURE: Colleagues by a coffee machine, one gesturing at him with a grin.

Colleagues sometimes called him J.P. As in J.P. Morgan.

---

## 3. The rules

PICTURE: Diagram: HEDGING. A mine sells copper today for delivery later.

Codelco had used the futures market since 1983, for one main job. Protection. A miner sells its future copper at today's price, so a crash can't hurt it.

PICTURE: A document, a typed board minute, a stamp.

In 1990, the company allowed something more. A small amount of real trading, just to make money. No more than ten thousand tonnes. And a strict loss limit.

PICTURE: Rule card: 20,000 TONNES · $1M A DAY · STOP-LOSS ON EVERY TRADE.

In August 1992, a committee set the rules. An open position of no more than twenty thousand tonnes. No more than a million dollars at risk on any day. And an automatic stop-loss order on every trade, to close it if it went wrong.

PICTURE: An empty meeting room, chairs pushed in, dust in the light.

According to the state auditors, it was the committee's last recorded meeting.

---

## 4. One man, one spreadsheet

PICTURE: A beige desktop computer, a spreadsheet glowing green on the screen.

Here is how it worked in practice. Dávila made the trades by phone. The brokers confirmed them by fax. And Dávila typed them into a spreadsheet.

That spreadsheet was the company's record. The accounts were built from it.

PICTURE: A fax machine curling out paper in a dark room.

He kept it on his office computer, and on his own personal computer. He used his own fax, and his own phone line.

PICTURE: A boxed IBM computer in a storeroom, unopened.

In 1991, Codelco had bought a proper system to control the trading. By April 1993 it was working. It was never used, because nobody typed in the old trades.

So the only person who knew what the trading desk was doing was the man running it.

---

## 5. The mistake

SCORE: SEPTEMBER 1993 · −$30,000,000

PICTURE: Chart: copper falling from $2,500 in mid-1992 to about $1,860 by September 1993.

By September 1993, copper had been falling for more than a year.

PICTURE: Close-up: a man's hand on a mouse, a cell in a spreadsheet changing.

That month, by Dávila's own account, he recorded a sale as a purchase. When he found the error, it had cost thirty million dollars.

PICTURE: The man sitting very still at his desk, the office emptying around him.

Thirty times the most he was allowed to risk in a single day. One phone call to his boss would have ended it.

He didn't make the call.

Instead, he decided to trade his way out. Quietly. Betting that copper would keep falling.

---

## 6. Digging

SCORE: NOVEMBER 1993 · COPPER $1,630

PICTURE: Diagram: SHORT. Sell now, buy back later. Falls are profit, rises are loss.

His bets were short positions. Sell now, buy back later. If the price falls, you profit. If it rises, you lose.

PICTURE: A wall of trading screens in London, then New York.

Over the next four months he made more than five thousand trades, with twenty-three brokers in London and New York. Copper, and then gold and silver too.

When one broker's credit ran out, he moved to another.

PICTURE: A beach house at dusk on the Pacific, a light on, a fax machine on a table.

Some of it, investigators later said, he ran from his beach house in Reñaca. He had the brokers send the paperwork to him, not to the office.

PICTURE: Chart: copper hits its low in November 1993, then turns up.

In November, copper hit its lowest price in years. Sixteen hundred and thirty dollars a tonne.

And then it turned.

---

## 7. The warnings

SCORE: JANUARY 1994 · COPPER $1,805

PICTURE: Two executives in a corridor, one tapping a sheet of paper.

That same November, two of his bosses heard that the desk was carrying positions for 1994 with losses of around ten million dollars.

PICTURE: A hand on a shoulder. A nod.

According to the auditors, they told him, verbally, to book the loss in that year's accounts.

He didn't. And nobody checked.

PICTURE: A broker's office in Hamburg, a man frowning at a screen.

On the other side of the trades, one broker was getting nervous. Wolfgang Becker, of Merrill Lynch, was one of Dávila's main brokers. Dávila was asking for more credit.

Becker later told the Wall Street Journal, I had the impression something was not right.

---

## 8. The confession

SCORE: 21 JANUARY 1994 · ~$100M (HIS ESTIMATE)

PICTURE: Callback: the coastal road, the corridor, the door.

Which brings us back to that Friday.

PICTURE: A sales manager's office, two men, the blinds half closed.

Dávila told the sales manager, Owen Guerrini, everything. The wrong key. The thirty million. The cover-up. The trades to win it back.

He thought the loss was now around a hundred million dollars.

PICTURE: An office lit at night over a weekend, executives in shirtsleeves.

Guerrini went straight up the chain. Emergency meetings ran all weekend. Codelco's heads in London and New York were flown home.

On Monday, the company filed a criminal complaint. Auditors from Price Waterhouse were called in. And Merrill Lynch's Becker helped Codelco unwind the positions.

PICTURE: Number card counting up: −$206,000,000.

It was worse than a hundred million. When Codelco closed the positions, the bill came to about two hundred and six million dollars.

Forty-four percent of the company's entire profit for 1993.

---

## 9. Coppergate

PICTURE: A press conference, cameras flashing, microphones on a table.

On the twenty-sixth of January, Codelco told the country. The executives above Dávila resigned. Ten days later, so did the head of the company.

PICTURE: Newspaper stacks at a Santiago kiosk.

The press called it Coppergate. The opposition threatened to impeach two ministers. Business groups said it proved Codelco should be sold.

PICTURE: Quote card: "HE WOULD HAVE TO BE A GENIUS."

One Codelco director said it was hard to believe one person could do all this without anyone else knowing. He would have to be a genius.

PICTURE: Dávila, at a window, calm.

Dávila said, I don't believe I've committed any crime. I made a mistake, and I am going to assume the responsibility.

PICTURE: Dictionary card: davilar (v., Chile): to botch things up miserably.

And Chile got a new word. Davilar. To botch things up miserably, as the Wall Street Journal translated it. When you get something catastrophically wrong in Chile, you don't just make a mistake. You davilar.

---

## 10. The other story

PICTURE: Bank statements on a table under a lamp, figures circled.

But investigators did not believe it was just a mistake.

PICTURE: Diagram: CODELCO → BROKER → CASH → DÁVILA, with ALLEGATION tag.

Codelco went through his bank accounts. It said that from 1990, large deposits had appeared in them, at least three point two million dollars by 1993, mostly in cash.

PICTURE: Bar chart: Codelco's trading with one London broker, 1990 to 1993.

Some of the money, Codelco said, came from accounts linked to the representatives of one London broker, Sogemin Metals. In 1990, its business with Codelco had been under two hundred thousand tonnes. In 1993, it was two point three million.

PICTURE: Map: Santiago to the Cayman Islands, a dotted line.

Codelco also said that in 1990, one and a half million dollars had moved from a subsidiary of the German metals giant Metallgesellschaft into an account in the Cayman Islands controlled by Dávila.

In lawsuits, Codelco alleged he had made losing trades for Codelco and winning trades for brokers, and shared the money.

PICTURE: A lawyer at courthouse steps.

Dávila denied it. His lawyer said the deposits were loans, and called him a scapegoat.

PICTURE: Quote card: JUDGE JOSÉ BENQUIS.

The judge who investigated the case, José Benquis, put it more bluntly. The story of the slip of the finger, he said, was so hard to swallow that he suspected at once there was something hidden.

---

## 11. The verdicts

SCORE: 2006 · CONVICTED

PICTURE: A Santiago courthouse.

In March 1994 he was charged. He would spend the next twelve years in Chile's courts.

PICTURE: Timeline card: 1999 · 8 YEARS → 2003 · 7 YEARS 7 MONTHS → 2006 · SUPREME COURT.

First he served a separate sentence for false tax returns. In 1999, a judge sentenced him to eight years for the losses. On appeal, it was cut. In 2006, the Supreme Court settled it.

Guilty of fraud against the state. And guilty of dealing with brokers where he had a conflict of interest. Three years, plus two terms of eight hundred days. And a hundred and eighty-six million dollars in damages to Codelco.

PICTURE: A prison gate in a small town, January light.

In January 2007, he handed himself in at a prison in the south of Chile.

PICTURE: Number card: $206M LOST · ~$100M RECOVERED.

By then, Codelco had sued and settled with brokers in London and the United States, and recovered about a hundred million dollars.

---

## 12. The wrong key

PICTURE: Chart: copper from 1992 to the end of 1994, his short bets marked, the price climbing to $3,000.

And here is the last twist.

Codelco later said that if copper had fallen, his trades would have made money. It didn't. Through 1994 it climbed to three thousand dollars a tonne. If he had held on, the losses would have been far bigger.

PICTURE: The beige computer, the spreadsheet, the cursor blinking.

Was it a slip of the little finger, followed by panic? Or a cover story for something else? A court decided. Chile never quite agreed.

PICTURE: The empty committee room again.

But the auditors found something that didn't depend on the answer. One man made the trades, recorded the trades and checked the trades. The committee had stopped meeting. The system that could have caught him sat switched off.

PICTURE: Graphic: the trade ticket again. BUY.

The wrong key cost thirty million dollars. Everything after that was a choice.

PICTURE: Sources card. Then end.
