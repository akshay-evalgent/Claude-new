# Sources for "How Did Ancient Humans Live Without Government?"

Checked on 2026-11-12 against the original papers, books, museum and university pages. The script follows the "safe to say" wording. The film is about anthropology and archaeology, not modern party politics.

| Claim in the film | Safe to say | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Countries today | 193 UN member states plus two observer states; any count of "countries" is disputed at the edges. | United Nations |
| Arnhem chimps | Becoming alpha took allies, not just muscle: the old alpha Yeroen lost to Luit, then backed the young Nikkie as alpha while keeping influence. Late 1970s. | de Waal, *Chimpanzee Politics*, 1982 |
| Mike at Gombe | In 1964 a low-ranking chimp, Mike, charged the top males while kicking and banging empty kerosene cans; they fled, came back and groomed him; he was alpha within about four months. He had lost two canines. | Goodall, *In the Shadow of Man*, 1971; National Geographic |
| Alpha styles | At Gombe the smallest of three alphas studied spent the most time grooming others. | Foster et al., *American Journal of Primatology* 2009 |
| Bonobo females | Female bonobos form coalitions, mostly against males, and older females back up younger ones. | Tokuyama & Furuichi, *Animal Behaviour* 2016; Surbeck et al., *Communications Biology* 2025 |
| Band size | A typical camp held a few dozen people, often about 25–50 (median about 27 in Marlowe's sample). | Marlowe, *Evolutionary Anthropology* 2005; Hill et al., *Science* 2011 |
| Band composition | In 32 foraging societies most band members were not close genetic relatives; adult siblings often co-resided and either sex could move. | Hill et al., *Science* 2011 |
| Hadza | No chiefs; camps have no fixed membership and change often; quarrels are often solved by moving. | Woodburn, *Man* 1982; Marlowe, *The Hadza*, 2010 |
| Ju/'hoansi waterhole owners | Families recognised as owners of a waterhole territory acted as hosts; nobody could give orders. | Marshall 1976; Lee 1979 |
| Reverse dominance hierarchy | From 48 societies: ordinary people team up to keep would-be bosses down with gossip, ridicule, disobedience, shunning, leaving and, rarely, killing. | Boehm, *Current Anthropology* 1993; *Hierarchy in the Forest*, 1999 |
| "Eating Christmas in the Kalahari" | Lee's ox was called a "bag of bones". Tomazo: "When a young man kills much meat he comes to think of himself as a chief or a big man… So we always speak of his meat as worthless. This way we cool his heart and make him gentle." /Gaugo described the modest hunter: "I saw nothing at all… just a little tiny one." | Lee, *Natural History*, December 1969 |
| Arrow ownership | Meat often belongs to the owner of the arrow, who may not be the hunter; arrows are lent and given, spreading the credit. Practice varies. | Lee 1979; Wiessner, *Evolution and Human Behavior* 2002 |
| Demand sharing | In many foraging societies sharing happens because people ask, and refusing a fair request is hard. | Peterson, *American Anthropologist* 1993 |
| Social talk | About two-thirds of relaxed conversation is about social topics; little of it is malicious. | Dunbar, Marriott & Duncan, *Human Nature* 1997 |
| Norm enforcement | 308 Ju/'hoansi conversations: more than half included criticism, praise was rare; joking and mockery came first, harsher criticism later; one show-off was refused meat and left for weeks, another shared a slaughtered cow to make peace. | Wiessner, *Human Nature* 2005 |
| Inuit song duels | In East Greenland two people in a quarrel could face off in a drum-song duel before the community, usually to clear the air; losing could bring shame, even exile. | Hoebel, *The Law of Primitive Man*, 1954 |
| !Kung homicide | 22 killings recorded 1920–1955, mostly from fights; in one remembered case a man who had killed several people was put to death by his community. | Lee, *The !Kung San*, 1979 |
| Self-domestication | Hypothesis: executing violent bullies made humans gentler; chimps fight within groups hundreds of times more often than people (his estimate 150–550x). | Wrangham, *The Goodness Paradox*, 2019 |
| Calusa | Fisher-gatherers whose ruler, called Carlos by the Spanish, took tribute from about 50 towns; met Governor Menéndez in 1566. | Fontaneda; Marquardt |
| Northwest Coast | Foragers with nobles, commoners and enslaved people, and potlatch feasts of giving. | Standard ethnography |
| Plains camp police | Some Plains nations appointed police for the summer bison hunt, whose power ended with the season. | Lowie 1948; Graeber & Wengrow, *The Dawn of Everything*, 2021 |
| Inuit seasons | Small family groups in summer, large shared winter houses with more sharing and ritual. | Mauss & Beuchat, *L'Année sociologique* 1906 |
| Nambikwara | The chief had no power to force anyone and kept followers by generosity and consent. | Lévi-Strauss 1944 |
| Amazonian chiefs | Clastres: chiefs as peacemakers, generous givers and fine speakers with no power to command; a commanding chief risked desertion. | Clastres, *Society Against the State*, 1974 |
| Melanesian big men | Influence earned by followers and feasts, not inherited; followers could drift away. | Sahlins, *Comparative Studies in Society and History* 1963 |
| House-size inequality | Median Gini about 0.17 for hunter-gatherers, 0.27 horticulturalists, 0.35 agriculturalists; much higher in the Old World with draft animals. | Kohler et al., *Nature* 2017 |
| Uruk | One of the first cities, 5,000–5,500 years ago; perhaps 40,000+ people by about 3000 BCE; walls about 6 km². | Archaeological surveys (summaries) |
| Early writing as accounting | The earliest Uruk tablets (c. 3300–3000 BCE) are mostly bookkeeping. | Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative |
| Egyptian cattle count | Old Kingdom officials counted herds about every two years for taxes; years named after the count. | Palermo Stone scholarship |
| Sumerian King List | "After the kingship descended from heaven…"; the first king Alulim "ruled 28,800 years". | ETCSL 2.1.1 |
| Law codes | Ur-Nammu, about 4,100 years ago; Hammurabi's about 282 laws on a black basalt stele over 2.25 m, found at Susa in 1901, now in the Louvre. | Louvre |
| Amargi | In Uruinimgina's reforms (about 2350 BCE), amargi, "return to the mother", meant release from debt slavery; often called one of the earliest written words for freedom. | Summaries of Uruinimgina's reforms |
| Grain and states | Scott argues early states relied on grain because it can be seen, measured, taxed and stored, and that many people fled them. An argument, debated. | Scott, *Against the Grain*, 2017 |
| Trypillia | About 6,000 years ago, giant ring settlements such as Nebelivka with large meeting buildings and no palaces or royal tombs; population hotly debated. | Gaydarska, Chapman et al. (Durham) |
| Teotihuacan | About 100 BCE–550 CE; more than 2,000 apartment compounds housed most of roughly 100,000 people; no royal tombs found; how it was ruled is debated. | Froese & Manzanilla, *PLOS One* 2014 |
