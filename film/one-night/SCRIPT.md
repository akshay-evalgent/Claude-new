# THE PHYSICS OF ONE NIGHT

A YouTube video essay with a short film inside it, directed the way Christopher Nolan would.
Running time about 13 minutes 30 seconds. Every number below comes from `physics/one_night.py`
and is printed in `physics/RESULTS.txt`. Change an assumption there and the script's numbers change with it.

**The question:** what would it really take for one person to deliver to every child on Earth in one night?

**The turn:** relativity doesn't give Santa more time. It takes some away.

**The prestige:** the trick works because there isn't one Santa. There are about a billion.

---

## How it's built

- **It opens at the end.** The first image is the last image, a pocket watch on a frosted windowsill at 6 a.m., as in *Memento*. The film then earns the right to show it again.
- **Every chapter card names a thing and a measure,** the way *Dunkirk* names The Mole, One Week. The measure on each card is that chapter's headline number.
- **One number per frame.** White type on black, or a number laid into a real image. No busy infographics.
- **Shoot it practical.** A real watch, real frost, real wrapping paper, a model sleigh on wires, candle flame for heat. Footage of real Earth from orbit is free from NASA.
- **One sound is the clock.** A sleigh bell ticks once a second through the whole film. Under it, a Shepard tone climbs through Chapters III and IV and stops dead at the start of Chapter V.
- **The narrator never says "Santa would die."** The numbers do that work. The tone stays calm, curious and a little in awe.

## COLD OPEN · 0:00

**PICTURE.** Black. Then a macro shot of a pocket watch lying on a frosted windowsill. Dawn light. The second hand moves. Frost creeps across the glass in timelapse. Rack focus to the window behind: a quiet street, snow, every window dark but one.

**SOUND.** The sleigh bell, once a second. Nothing else.

**NARRATION.**
> Suppose this watch went around the world last night, to every home on Earth.
>
> It would now be half a second slow.
>
> Half a second is the only mark the trip would leave. To understand why, we have to work out what one night actually costs.

**PICTURE.** Hard cut to black.

**TITLE CARD.** THE PHYSICS OF ONE NIGHT

## I · THE LIST · 2,020,000,000 · 0:50

**PICTURE.** A single lit window at night, seen from across a street. Slow pull back. More windows. A whole city. Then real orbital footage of Earth's night side, every city a smear of light.

**NARRATION.**
> In January 1990, *Spy* magazine ran a joke calculation called "Is There a Santa Claus?" It counted 91.8 million homes and gave him 31 hours. That works out to 822.6 visits a second.
>
> The world has changed since 1990, so let's count again.
>
> The World Bank's latest figures put the number of children under fifteen at just over two billion. Say there are two children in a typical home that has any. That's one billion homes.
>
> If he only visits homes that celebrate Christmas, the list is smaller. Using Pew's figure that 29 percent of the world is Christian, it drops to about 290 million homes. That's still three times the 1990 list. We'll take the full version, because that's the promise. Every child.

**ON SCREEN.** 2,020,000,000 children · 1,010,000,000 homes

---

## II · THE NIGHT · 33 HOURS · 2:40

**PICTURE.** A globe, practical and spinning slowly on a desk lamp's light. The terminator line between day and night crawls across it. A finger rests on a tiny island in the Pacific.

**NARRATION.**
> The night doesn't arrive everywhere at once. It sweeps west.
>
> The first inhabited place to reach Christmas Eve is Kiritimati, in Kiribati, fourteen hours ahead of Greenwich. The last is American Samoa, eleven hours behind. That's 25 hours of time zones.
>
> Give him eight hours of darkness in each place, from ten at night to six in the morning. If he sweeps west with the night, he has 33 hours in total.
>
> One billion homes in 33 hours is about 8,500 homes every second.
>
> He has 118 millionths of a second at each one.

**SOUND.** The sleigh bell keeps its tick. Under it, very quietly, the Shepard tone begins.

**ON SCREEN.** 8,500 homes per second · 118 µs per home

---

## III · THE ROUTE · 94,500,000 KM · 4:00

**PICTURE.** Pins in a paper map, joined by red thread. The thread is pulled tight between two pins. Push in until the thread fills the frame.

**NARRATION.**
> How far does he travel? If you scatter points across a map, mathematicians have a formula for the shortest route through all of them. The route gets shorter where the points crowd together, in cities, and longer where they spread out, across farmland.
>
> We ran that formula for all 217 countries and territories in the World Bank data, with each country's homes split between its towns and its countryside.
>
> The shortest route is 94.5 million kilometres. That's 2,360 times around the Earth, or nearly two-thirds of the way to the Sun.
>
> To cover it in 33 hours, he has to average 795 kilometres a second. That's Mach 2,300.
>
> It's faster than the speed needed to escape the Milky Way from here, which is roughly 550 kilometres a second. At that speed, the Earth's surface curves away beneath him so fast that he'd need a force of ten thousand g just to stay on the planet.

**SOUND.** The Shepard tone is rising now.

**ON SCREEN.** 795 km/s · Mach 2,300 · 1.45× galactic escape speed

---

## IV · THE BODY · 2,750,000,000 G · 5:50

**PICTURE.** A model sleigh on fine wires in a black studio, practical snow drifting past it. A single wrapped present on a kitchen scale reads 1.00 kg.

**NARRATION.**
> He doesn't just fly past. He stops. At every home.
>
> The average distance between homes on his route is 94 metres. He has to cover it, stop, and start again, 8,500 times a second. That takes an acceleration of 2.75 billion g.
>
> Fighter pilots in g-suits train to withstand about nine.
>
> Then there are the presents. Give every child a one-kilogram gift and he leaves the North Pole carrying two million tonnes.
>
> Speeding up and slowing down that load, a billion times, takes about 2.6 times ten to the thirty joules. That's more energy than humanity would use in four billion years, which is almost the age of the Earth. It's what the Sun puts out, in every direction, in almost two hours.
>
> And the air is in the way. Pushing through it at 795 kilometres a second takes 1.8 exawatts. That's nearly a hundred thousand times the power of all human civilisation, delivered to the front of a sleigh.

**PICTURE.** A candle flame. A single fleck of snow drifts into it and vanishes. Hold.

**SOUND.** The Shepard tone reaches the top of its climb.

**ON SCREEN.** 2,750,000,000 g · 2,000,000 tonnes · 4,300,000,000 years of human energy

---

## V · THE CLOCK · −0.557 SECONDS · 7:40

**SOUND.** The Shepard tone cuts out. Only the sleigh bell remains.

**PICTURE.** Archive photographs of 1971: two physicists and a set of atomic clocks strapped into airliner seats.

**NARRATION.**
> In 1971, two physicists, Joseph Hafele and Richard Keating, bought plane tickets for four atomic clocks and flew them around the world. Once eastward, once westward. When the clocks came home, they no longer agreed with the clocks that stayed behind.
>
> Flying east, they had lost 59 billionths of a second. Flying west, they had gained 273. Einstein had predicted it. Moving clocks run slow, and clocks higher in a gravity field run fast.
>
> So run Santa's night through Einstein.

**PICTURE.** Back to the pocket watch, macro. The second hand stops.

**NARRATION.**
> Flying at those speeds for 33 hours, his watch falls behind by 0.557 seconds. Flying a kilometre up gives him back 13 billionths of a second. That's nothing.
>
> This is the part most people get backwards. Relativity doesn't give him more time. Moving fast makes his clock run slow, so he experiences less of the night than we do.
>
> The faster he goes, the less time he gets.

**ON SCREEN.** −0.557 s

**PICTURE.** Black for two full seconds. The bell ticks twice in the dark.

---

## VI · THE PRICE · 23 YEARS · 9:00

**PICTURE.** Real starfield. A slow push toward a black disc with a thin bright ring around it. Practical: a dark sphere and a ring of light painted on glass, shot through a long lens.

**NARRATION.**
> There's one way relativity could make his night feel short. Not short for him, but long for everyone else.
>
> Suppose one night passes on Santa's watch while 23 years pass at the North Pole. For that, his clock has to run 6,110 times slower than ours.
>
> In *Interstellar*, one hour on Miller's planet is seven years back on Earth. That's a factor of about 61,000. Santa needs a tenth of that.
>
> To get it by speed alone, he'd have to fly at 99.9999987 percent of the speed of light. Getting an empty one-tonne sleigh to that speed takes as much energy as humanity would use in nine hundred years.
>
> And here is the catch. The North Pole and the children run on the same clock. It's the Earth's clock. If 23 years pass at home, 23 years pass for every child on his list.
>
> He would deliver every present on time, by his watch, to two billion adults.

**TITLE CARD.** ONE NIGHT

## VII · THE PRESTIGE · 1,000,000,000 · 11:00

**PICTURE.** A magician's hands on black velvet, practical. An empty top hat. A wrapped present appears inside it.

**NARRATION.**
> Every magic trick has three parts. The pledge shows you something ordinary. The turn makes it do something impossible. The third part, the hardest, is bringing it back. That's the prestige.
>
> The pledge was a list of a billion homes. The turn was one night. Physics says the turn can't be done. Not at 795 kilometres a second, not at the speed of light, not by one person.
>
> So how is it done?
>
> If nobody breaks the laws of physics, and you drive at thirty kilometres an hour, and spend ten minutes at each home, you need about 21 million helpers working through the night.

**PICTURE.** Real, unstaged footage, gathered from crew and family: a hallway at 2 a.m. A parent in socks carrying presents. A bitten carrot. A note in a child's handwriting. A hand placing a stocking. A tired face lit by a phone torch.

**NARRATION.**
> There aren't 21 million.
>
> There are about a billion. One in nearly every home on the list. Each of them gives up a night.
>
> Every one of them breaks no laws of physics at all.

**PICTURE.** The windowsill from the cold open. The same pocket watch, the same frost, the same dawn. A parent's hand, in a dressing gown sleeve, picks it up and winds it.

**NARRATION.**
> This watch didn't go anywhere last night.
>
> It's exactly on time.

**SOUND.** The bell ticks once more. Then silence.

**PICTURE.** Black.

**TITLE CARD.** ONE NIGHT

**END CARD, held for 4 seconds.** Every calculation in this film is in the description.

## What the numbers depend on

| Assumption | Used | Range tested | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Children per home | 2.0 | 1.5 to 3.0 | Speed 649 to 918 km/s, watch slow by 0.37 to 0.74 s |
| Urban density | 5,000 people per km² | 2,500 to 10,000 | Speed 757 to 849 km/s |
| Who's on the list | Every child under 15 | Christian homes only | 427 km/s, 0.16 s slow |

The headline survives every version. He is always hundreds of kilometres a second too fast for the air, billions of g too violent for a body, and his watch always ends the night a fraction of a second behind.
