# Sources for "Why Did Humans Start Wearing Clothes?"

Checked on 2026-11-15 against the original papers, museum and university pages. The script uses the "safe to say" wording, and hypotheses are spoken as hypotheses.

| Claim in the film | Safe to say | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Clothing sold | More than 100 billion units sold a year by 2015, about double 2000 (sales, not production). | Ellen MacArthur Foundation, *A New Textiles Economy*, 2017 (Euromonitor data) |
| US per person | About 69 garments per person in 2021 (supply ÷ population). | American Apparel & Footwear Association data |
| Hair follicles | Human skin has chimpanzee-like hair-follicle density; most human hairs are fine (small sample). | Kamberov et al., *Journal of Human Evolution* 2018 |
| Sweat glands | About 10× the eccrine gland density of chimps; 2–4 million glands; humans can sweat 1 litre or more an hour. | Kamberov et al. 2018; Aldea et al., *PNAS* 2021; Baker, *Temperature* 2019 |
| Why fur was lost | Leading idea: cooling by sweat while active in hot, open country; not proven. Homo erectus/ergaster probably among the first with bare skin, by about 1.6 million years ago. | Wheeler 1984, 1991; Bramble & Lieberman, *Nature* 2004; Jablonski, *Scientific American* 2010 |
| Dark skin | MC1R selection for dark skin about 1.2 million years ago implies mostly bare skin by then, possibly earlier. | Rogers, Iltis & Wooding, *Current Anthropology* 2004 |
| Clothing lice | Live and lay eggs on clothing, move to skin to feed; split from head lice at least 83,000 and possibly 170,000 years ago (wide range). Stoneking's team first dated the split in 2003. | CDC; Kittler, Kayser & Stoneking, *Current Biology* 2003; Toups et al., *Molecular Biology and Evolution* 2011 |
| Bare for a long time | Mostly bare by about 1.2 million years ago, but good clothing clues only 100,000–170,000 years old; people in warm Africa may have managed with little or no clothing for hundreds of thousands of years (organic remains rarely survive). | Inference from the sources above |
| Ice Age cold | Gilligan argues clothing began mainly as protection from glacial cold, and that the need for cloth may have helped lead to farming (debated). | Gilligan, *J. Archaeological Method and Theory* 2010; Gilligan, *Climate, Clothing, and Agriculture in Prehistory*, 2018 |
| Contrebandiers Cave | 62 bone tools for working leather and fur, about 90,000–120,000 years old; skinning cut marks on sand fox, golden jackal and wildcat. | Hallett et al., *iScience* 2021 |
| Neanderthal cord | Three-ply cord from Abri du Maras, France, about 41,000–52,000 years old. | Hardy et al., *Scientific Reports* 2020 |
| Eyed needles | Oldest about 40,000 years (Denisova Cave, Siberia); earlier people used bone awls. | Gilligan, d'Errico et al., *Science Advances* 2024 |
| Woven impressions | Woven material pressed into clay at Pavlov I, Czech Republic, about 26,000 years ago. | Adovasio, Soffer & Klíma, *Antiquity* 1996 |
| Yana | People north of the Arctic Circle about 32,000 years ago; nearly 200 needles and fragments. | Pitulko et al., *Science* 2004; Pitulko & Pavlova |
| Neanderthal clothing | Worked hides; a model suggests some groups covered up to ~80% of the body in winter, probably with draped, untailored clothing (educated guesswork). | Wales, *J. Human Evolution* 2012; Collard et al., *J. Anthropological Archaeology* 2016 |
| Toe bones | Weaker little-toe bones suggest regular footwear by about 40,000 years ago (Tianyuan). | Trinkaus 2005; Trinkaus & Shang, *J. Archaeological Science* 2008 |
| Dzudzuana fibres | Wild flax fibres from more than 30,000 years ago, some spun; some apparently coloured black, grey, turquoise, and one pink in a later layer (identification questioned by some). | Kvavadze et al., *Science* 2009; Bergfjord et al. 2010 |
| Indigo | Oldest known indigo-dyed cotton, Huaca Prieta, Peru, about 6,000 years old. | Splitstoser et al., *Science Advances* 2016 |
| Venus figurines | Willendorf (about 29,500 years) may wear a woven cap or braided hair; Lespugue (about 25,000 years) a string skirt. | Soffer, Adovasio & Hyland, *Current Anthropology* 2000; Barber 1994 |
| Sungir | A man and two boys, about 34,000 years ago, with more than 13,000 ivory beads apparently sewn onto clothing; about an hour per bead by one estimate. | Trinkaus & Buzhilova, *Antiquity* 2018; Sikora et al., *Science* 2017; White 1993 |
| Shell beads | Bizmoune Cave, Morocco: at least 142,000 years old, the oldest known. | Sehasseh et al., *Science Advances* 2021 |
| Identity | Clothing became a tool for group identity and status. | Gilligan et al., *Science Advances* 2024 |
| Ötzi | About 5,300 years ago: bearskin hat, sheep-and-goat coat, goatskin leggings, cattle-leather laces, deerskin-topped shoes stuffed with hay/grass; at least five animal species. | O'Sullivan et al., *Scientific Reports* 2016; South Tyrol Museum of Archaeology |
| Replica shoes | A shoemaker who hiked in replicas said they beat most modern footwear (press report). | Petr Hlaváček, via Bata Shoe Museum |
| Tarkhan Dress | Linen shirt with V-neck and pleated sleeves, about 5,000 years old (3482–3102 BC); excavated 1913, recognised 1977 in a bundle of linen. | Stevenson & Dee, *Antiquity* 2016; UCL Petrie Museum |
| Trousers | Among the oldest known, about 3,000 years old, Yanghai, China, worn by horse riders. | Beck et al., *Quaternary International* 2014 |
| Areni-1 shoe | One-piece leather shoe, about 5,500 years old, laced, packed with grass (probably to keep its shape), about EU size 37. | Pinhasi et al., *PLoS ONE* 2010 |
| Fort Rock sandals | Sagebrush-bark sandals, about 9,000–10,500 years old. | University of Oregon Museum of Natural and Cultural History |
