# Sources for "What Did Ancient Humans Do for Fun Before Phones?"

Checked on 2026-11-14 against the original papers, books, museum and university pages. The script uses the "safe to say" wording; hypotheses are spoken as hypotheses.

| Claim in the film | Safe to say | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Phone time | Many of us spend around four hours a day on our phones: worldwide mobile internet time is about 3 h 46 min a day, and US estimates run 4–5 hours. | DataReportal/GWI, Digital 2025; eMarketer 2025 |
| Rats "laugh" | Tickled young rats make ~50 kHz chirps and come back to the hand for more; Panksepp called it laughter-like (a working hypothesis). | Panksepp & Burgdorf, *Behavioural Brain Research* 2000; *Physiology & Behavior* 2001, 2003; Ishiyama & Brecht, *Science* 2016 |
| Ape laughter | Tickled orangutans, gorillas, chimps, bonobos and human babies; laughter is homologous in great apes and humans, from a shared ancestor roughly 10–16 million years ago. | Davila Ross, Owren & Zimmermann, *Current Biology* 2009 |
| Stick "dolls" | Young female chimps at Kanyawara (Kibale, Uganda) carry sticks, rest with them and take them into nests; it stopped once they had babies (possible play-mothering). | Kahlenberg & Wrangham, *Current Biology* 2010 |
| Agta free time | Mostly-farming Agta work about 30 h a week outside camp vs about 20 h for mostly-foraging Agta: roughly 10 hours' less free time. | Dyble et al., *Nature Human Behaviour* 2019 |
| Ju/'hoansi work | Lee: about 2.5 days a week getting food (1964); about 40–44 h a week with all work counted (1979). Critique of the "affluent society" idea noted. | Lee 1968, *Man the Hunter*; Lee 1979, *The !Kung San*; Kaplan 2000 |
| Firelight talk | By day: 31% economic talk, 34% complaints, stories in 7% of conversations. At night, 81% of long group conversations were stories. | Wiessner, *PNAS* 2014 |
| Agta storytellers | Skilled storytellers nominated as campmates nearly twice as often (odds ratio 1.95), ahead of hunting and fishing; 0.53 more living children on average (correlation). Stories stress cooperation and equality between the sexes. | Smith et al., *Nature Communications* 2017 |
| Hadza gambling | Lukuchuko: men toss bark discs at a tree and bet items such as arrows; winners keep playing, so prized items circulate. | Woodburn, *Man* 1982; Pickles 2016, *Open Encyclopedia of Anthropology* |
| Knucklebones | Sheep and goat ankle bones used in games for thousands of years; Maresha (Israel) cache of 600+, about 2,300 years old, some inscribed with game words such as "Stop!". Still played in Mongolia. | British Museum 1867,0510.1; Perry-Gal et al., *Levant* 2022; UNESCO (Mongolian knuckle-bone shooting) |
| Başur Höyük | 49 carved and painted stones (pigs, dogs, pyramids) in a grave of about 3100–2900 BC; possibly among the oldest game pieces. | Hassett & Sağlamtimur, *Antiquity* 2018 |
| Senet | Played in Egypt more than 4,500 years ago (Hesy-Re, about 2620 BC), roots possibly about 5,000 years; later tied to the afterlife journey; Tutankhamun buried with at least four game boxes. | Piccione 1980; Tait 1982; Crist, Dunn-Vaturi & de Voogt 2016 |
| Royal Game of Ur | Boards from Ur's Royal Cemetery, about 2600–2400 BC; rules reconstructed by Irving Finkel from a tablet of about 177 BC by the scribe Itti-Marduk-balāṭu; played for well over 2,000 years; a descendant played in Kochi, India, until the 1950s; board scratched into a Khorsabad lamassu base, possibly by guards. | Finkel 2007, *Ancient Board Games in Perspective*; British Museum |
| Indus toys | Clay carts, wheeled animals and nodding-head animals, about 2600–1900 BC; may have been toys. | CSMVS Mumbai; harappa.com (J. M. Kenoyer) |
| Hunter-gatherer toys | Children make small bows, digging sticks and dolls, copying adults. | Lancy, *The Anthropology of Childhood*, 2015 |
| Spinning disc | Laugerie-Basse bone disc with an animal standing on one side and lying on the other; about 14,000 years old; may have been spun as a "flip toy", or used as a tool. | Azéma & Rivère, *Antiquity* 2012; Needham et al., *EXARC* 2019 |
| String figures | Diamond Jenness recorded well over a hundred Inuit string figures in 1913–16. | Jenness 1924, *Report of the Canadian Arctic Expedition* |
| Cave of Swimmers | Wadi Sura paintings about 6,500–8,500 years old, from a greener Sahara; "swimming" reading contested. | Kuper (ed.) 2013, *Wadi Sura – The Cave of Beasts* |
| Beni Hasan | Tomb of Baqet III: about 220 wrestling pairs, about 4,000 years old; also the earliest known picture of juggling. | Egyptian Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities; Kanawati 2011 |
| Rubber balls | El Manatí balls about 3,600–3,700 years old; latex mixed with morning-glory juice for bounce. | Ortiz & Rodríguez (El Manatí); Hosler, Burkett & Tarkanian, *Science* 1999 |
| Blanket toss | Iñupiat celebrate a good whaling season (Nalukataq) with the blanket toss on walrus or seal skins; one-foot high kick still contested. | North Slope Borough; Arctic Winter Games; World Eskimo-Indian Olympics |
| Flutes | Bird-bone and mammoth-ivory flutes from caves in southwest Germany, about 40,000 years old; the Hohle Fels vulture-bone flute has five finger holes. | Conard, Malina & Münzel, *Nature* 2009; Higham et al., *Journal of Human Evolution* 2012 |
| Cave echoes | In some Ice Age caves, art tends to sit where sound echoes or resonates; debated. | Reznikoff & Dauvois 1988; Waller, *Nature* 1993; Fazenda et al., *JASA* 2017 |
| Dance | Some of the oldest dance scenes come from early farming villages of the Middle East, about 9,000 years ago. | Garfinkel, *Cambridge Archaeological Journal* 1998; Garfinkel 2003 |
| Aka singing | Aka polyphonic singing proclaimed a UNESCO Masterpiece in 2003; on the Representative List since 2008. | UNESCO ICH element 00082 |
| Oldest joke | Sumerian proverb, at least 1900 BC: "Something which has never occurred since time immemorial: a young woman did not fart in her husband's embrace." | ETCSL Proverbs collection 1, 1.12; University of Wolverhampton (McDonald) 2008 |
| Philogelos | Greek joke book, 4th–5th century AD, 264–265 jokes; barber joke (no. 148), told earlier by Plutarch of King Archelaus of Macedon; egghead (scholastikos) jokes. | Berg 2008 (translation); Plutarch, *Moralia* 177A |
| "Boredom" | First found in English print in 1829; popularised by Dickens's *Bleak House* (1852–53). | Merriam-Webster; Dictionary.com |
| "Just think" | People left alone for 15 minutes to think; many chose to give themselves a mild electric shock (67% of men, 25% of women). | Wilson et al., *Science* 2014 |
