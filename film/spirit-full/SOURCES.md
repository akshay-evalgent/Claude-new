# Sources for "What Did Ancient Humans Believe In?"

Checked on 2026-11-12 against the original papers, museum pages and university releases. The script follows the "safe to say" wording. The film describes what archaeology shows people did; it makes no claim about whether any belief is true.

| Claim in the film | Safe to say | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Religious affiliation today | In 2020 about 76% of people worldwide identified with a religion. | Pew Research Center, "How the Global Religious Landscape Changed From 2010 to 2020", 2025 |
| Human universals | Ritual, mourning, death rituals and belief in the supernatural are recorded in every culture ethnographers have described. | Brown, *Human Universals*, 1991 |
| Elephants and remains | Wild elephants at Amboseli spent more time sniffing and touching elephant skulls and ivory than other skulls; ivory drew the most interest. | McComb, Baker & Moss, *Biology Letters* 2006 |
| Chimp mothers | At Bossou, Guinea, two mothers carried their dead infants for 19 and 68 days, grooming them and keeping flies away. | Biro et al., *Current Biology* 2010 |
| Pansy | At Blair Drummond Safari Park (2008) the group groomed the dying Pansy and checked for signs of life; her daughter Rosie stayed near the body overnight; no chimp slept on that platform for five days. | Anderson, Gillies & Lock, *Current Biology* 2010 |
| Waterfall displays | Goodall wondered whether chimps' waterfall displays are stirred by something like wonder or awe (speculation). | Goodall, "Primate spirituality", *Encyclopedia of Religion and Nature* 2005 |
| Stone throwing | Chimps in four West African populations throw stones at particular trees, leaving piles; purpose unknown; resemblance to human cairns noted. | Kühl et al., *Scientific Reports* 2016 |
| Homo naledi | Researchers argue Homo naledi (remains about 236,000–335,000 years old) placed bodies in dug graves; many scientists remain unconvinced. | Berger et al., *eLife* 2023/2025; Martinón-Torres et al., *J. Human Evolution* 2024 |
| Qafzeh | About 90,000–100,000 years ago, a 12–13-year-old was buried with deer antlers against the chest; a healed head injury from years earlier. | Coqueugniot et al., *PLOS ONE* 2014 |
| Mtoto | Panga ya Saidi, Kenya, about 78,000 years: a child of about 2.5–3 laid on its side, knees drawn up, probably shrouded; head probably on a perishable support. Oldest known modern-human burial in Africa. | Martinón-Torres et al., *Nature* 2021 |
| La Chapelle-aux-Saints | Researchers argue this Neanderthal (about 50,000 years) was buried in a dug pit; most accept it, some dispute it. | Rendu et al., *PNAS* 2014; Dibble et al. 2015 |
| Shanidar pollen | The "flower burial" pollen was most likely brought in by ground-nesting bees; Neanderthals there seem to have laid their dead in one particular place. | Hunt et al., *J. Archaeological Science* 2023; Pomeroy et al., *Antiquity* 2020 |
| Bruniquel | About 176,000 years ago, Neanderthals broke about 400 stalagmites into rings and piles about 336 m inside the cave; all carry traces of fire; purpose unknown. | Jaubert et al., *Nature* 2016 |
| Early pigment | By about 300,000 years ago at Olorgesailie, Kenya, people processed red, iron-rich rock, probably for colour. | Brooks et al., *Science* 2018 |
| Blombos | Engraved crosshatched ochre from about 100,000–75,000 years ago; a drawing in ochre crayon on a stone flake about 73,000 years old, the oldest known drawing; lines run off the edge. | Henshilwood et al., *Science* 2002; *J. Human Evolution* 2009; *Nature* 2018 |
| Red Lady of Paviland | Found in 1823; really a young man, buried about 33,000 years ago with red ochre and ivory ornaments. | Jacobi & Higham, *J. Human Evolution* 2008 |
| Sungir | About 34,000 years ago, two boys buried head to head with more than 10,000 mammoth-ivory beads, a man with about 3,000; thousands of hours of work; spears of straightened mammoth tusk. | Trinkaus & Buzhilova, *Antiquity* 2018; White 1993 |
| Sulawesi | Leang Karampuang: at least 51,200 years, three human-like figures around a pig, the oldest known story scene. Leang Bulu' Sipong 4 hunting scene with part-animal figures, at least about 48,000 years. | Oktaviana et al., *Nature* 2024; Aubert et al., *Nature* 2019 |
| Lion-Man | About 40,000 years old, mammoth ivory, about 31 cm, found in hundreds of fragments; hundreds of hours to carve (estimates about 320–400+). | Museum Ulm |
| Flutes | A griffon-vulture wing-bone flute with five finger holes, about 40,000 years old, among the oldest known instruments. | Conard, Malina & Münzel, *Nature* 2009 |
| Lascaux | In the Shaft, a bird-headed stick man falls back before a wounded bison, the cave's only human figure; about 21,000 years old. | French Ministry of Culture, Lascaux dating |
| Trois-Frères "Sorcerer" | A part-human, part-animal figure high above a chamber of engraved animals; roughly 15,000 years old (not directly dated). | Begouën & Breuil; secondary summaries |
| Cave acoustics | In some caves paintings cluster where the voice resonates; later studies found the link weak; some stalactites show wear suggesting they were struck. Unproven. | Reznikoff & Dauvois, *BSPF* 1988; later critiques |
| Ju/'hoansi healing dance | Women clap and sing, men dance, sometimes all night; healers describe n/um "boiling" up the spine; roughly half of adult men become healers, fewer women. | Katz, *Boiling Energy*, 1982 |
| San rock art and trance | Lewis-Williams argued much southern African rock art reflects trance experience; influential but criticised. | Lewis-Williams, various |
| Hilazon Tachtit | About 12,000 years ago, a small elderly woman with a disability buried with 50 tortoise shells and parts of rare animals (eagle, leopard, wild boar, cow's tail), with a feast; the excavators think she may have been a shaman. | Grosman, Munro & Belfer-Cohen, *PNAS* 2008; Munro & Grosman, *PNAS* 2010 |
| Hunter-gatherer beliefs | In 33 hunter-gatherer societies: animism 100%, afterlife 79%, shamanism 79%, ancestor worship 45%, high gods 39%, high gods active in human affairs 15%. Recent foragers, not a direct window on the Ice Age. | Peoples, Duda & Marlowe, *Human Nature* 2016 |
| Çatalhöyük | About 9,000 years ago people buried their dead beneath the floors of their houses, sometimes under sleeping platforms. | Çatalhöyük Research Project |
| Jericho | About 9,000 years ago some skulls were given plaster faces with shells for eyes. | Kenyon 1953; museum collections |
| Göbekli Tepe | Begun about 11,500 years ago by hunter-gatherers; tallest pillars about 5.5 m and up to about 10 t; foxes, boars, snakes, vultures; some pillars have carved arms; Schmidt: "first came the temple, then the city"; houses and rock-cut cisterns show people also lived there. | German Archaeological Institute (DAI) |
| Newgrange | About 3200 BC; at the winter solstice, sunrise enters a roof-box and lights the chamber for up to about 17 minutes; older than Stonehenge's stone circle and the Giza pyramids. | National Museum of Ireland; OPW |
| Stonehenge | Sarsens raised about 2500 BC, aligned on midsummer sunrise and midwinter sunset; bluestones from the Preseli Hills, more than 200 km; the six-tonne Altar Stone from north-east Scotland, at least 750 km. | Clarke et al., *Nature* 2024; English Heritage |
| Durrington Walls | Pigs mostly slaughtered in winter; isotopes show some were raised as far away as Scotland, north-east England and west Wales. | Madgwick et al., *Science Advances* 2019 |
| Dancing in sync | In a "silent disco" study, dancing in sync raised pain thresholds (a sign of endorphin release) and closeness. | Tarr, Launay & Dunbar, *Evolution and Human Behavior* 2016 |
| Kavadi | Men who took part in or watched a painful ritual gave more money anonymously to the temple than those who only joined prayers. | Xygalatas et al., *Psychological Science* 2013 |
| Ain Mallaha | About 12,000 years ago, an older person was buried with a hand resting on a puppy. | Davis & Valla, *Nature* 1978 |
| Bonn-Oberkassel | About 14,000 years ago a man and a woman were buried with a dog that died at about 27–28 weeks; teeth record repeated illness (probably distemper); it likely survived only with human care. | Janssens et al., *J. Archaeological Science* 2018 |
