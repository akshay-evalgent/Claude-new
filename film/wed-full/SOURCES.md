# Sources for "How Did Ancient Humans Get Married?"

Checked on 2026-11-13 against the original papers, books, museum and university pages. The script follows the "safe to say" wording and describes every marriage system neutrally.

| Claim in the film | Safe to say | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Wedding cost | The Knot's 2026 study (about 10,000 couples married in 2025): average about $34,000, about 117 guests, about $292 per guest. The median is much lower. | The Knot, 2026 Real Weddings Study |
| Marriage everywhere | Marriage is among the traits reported in every known culture. | Brown, *Human Universals*, 1991 |
| The caveman cliché | Grew from Victorian "marriage by capture" theories (John McLennan, 1865, who never studied a hunter-gatherer society) and novels around 1900; films such as Buster Keaton's *The Three Ages* (1923) made it a joke. No archaeological basis. | JSTOR Daily; Ruddick, *Yearbook of English Studies* 2007 |
| Apes | Gibbons usually live as pairs (not always faithful); gorillas usually one adult male with several females (some groups have more males); chimps and bonobos in mixed groups. Male/female weight: gorilla about 2–2.4x, chimp about 1.3x, human about 1.15x. | Plavcan 2001 (via CARTA); Reichard & Boesch 2003 |
| Voles | Prairie voles often form long-lasting pairs; adding vasopressin receptors to one reward area of male meadow voles' brains made them prefer a familiar partner. | Lim et al., *Nature* 2004 |
| Romantic love | Evidence in 147 of 166 cultures; gaps likely in the records. | Jankowiak & Fischer, *Ethnology* 1992 |
| Ain Sakhri figurine | About 11,000 years old, near Bethlehem, carved from one pebble about 10 cm tall; two people in a close embrace; often called the oldest known depiction of a couple. | British Museum, 1958,1007.1 |
| Aka fathers | Fathers are within arm's reach of their babies for about half of each day; that care may have favoured pair bonds (hypothesis). | Hewlett, *Intimate Fathers*, 1991 |
| Hadza marriage | Usually not arranged, no ceremony; married once living together; divorce fairly common; most adults marry more than once; about 4% of marriages have more than one wife. | Marlowe, *The Hadza*, 2010; Blurton Jones 2016 |
| Hadza preferences | Both sexes stressed good character; women also valued intelligence and foraging; men valued hard work; body size hardly mattered (1 of 46 women said "big"). | Marlowe, *Human Nature* 2004 |
| Bride service | Ju/'hoansi husbands lived with the wife's family and hunted for them, often several years, sometimes up to ten; then the couple chose where to live. | Lee, *The Dobe Ju/'hoansi*; Marshall, *Africa* 1959 |
| Nisa | First marriages often arranged; Nisa ran away from early husbands; divorce possible and often started by the wife; close to half of first marriages ended in divorce. | Shostak, *Nisa*, 1981 |
| In-law avoidance | Dyirbal speakers (Queensland) avoided direct talk and eye contact with certain in-laws and used a special "mother-in-law" vocabulary (Jalnguy) with the same grammar. | Dixon 1980; Dixon 2025 |
| Sunghir DNA | Four people buried about 34,000 years ago were no more closely related than second cousins: small bands within wider mating networks. | Sikora et al., *Science* 2017 |
| Chagyrskaya Neanderthals | 13 individuals about 54,000 years old, including a father and teenage daughter; communities of about 10–20; women more often moved between communities. | Skov et al., *Nature* 2022 |
| Denny | A girl at least 13, about 90,000 years ago, with a Neanderthal mother and Denisovan father; known from one bone fragment; her father had some Neanderthal ancestry. | Slon et al., *Nature* 2018 |
| Gurgy | About 6,700 years ago: DNA from 94 people, a 64-person tree over seven generations; patrilineal; most women from elsewhere; adult daughters largely absent; no half-siblings. | Rivollat et al., *Nature* 2023 |
| Hazleton North | About 5,700 years ago: 27 of 35 people close relatives over five generations; most descended from one man and four women with whom he had children. | Fowler et al., *Nature* 2021 |
| Lech valley | About 4,000 years ago, most women had grown up far away; often buried with rich grave goods; households included unrelated poorer people. | Knipper et al., *PNAS* 2017; Mittnik et al., *Science* 2019 |
| Marriage rules | Of 1,231 societies: about 85% allowed more than one wife, about 15% monogamy only, 4 allowed more than one husband; most men in polygynous societies had one wife; later work found dozens more with occasional polyandry. | Murdock, *Ethnographic Atlas*; Starkweather & Hames 2012 |
| Nyinba | Brothers traditionally married one wife together, keeping the farm undivided. | Levine, *The Dynamics of Polyandry*, 1988 |
| Mosuo | "Walking marriage": separate homes, the man visits at night, children raised in the mother's household often with uncles; changing today. | Cai Hua, *A Society without Fathers or Husbands*, 2001 |
| Marriage gifts | Bridewealth in about two-thirds of societies; dowry in fewer than 4%. | Gaulin & Boster, *American Anthropologist* 1990 |
| Kültepe contract | About 4,000 years ago, Assyrian merchants at Kültepe (Turkey) wrote a marriage agreement covering what would happen with no child in two years. | Turp et al., *Gynecological Endocrinology* 2017 |
| Hammurabi | About 3,750 years ago, rules for marriage gifts, dowry and divorce. | Code of Hammurabi |
| Egypt | Marriage a private arrangement ("founding a house"); women kept their property, either partner could divorce, and a divorced woman took her property plus a share of joint gains. | University of Chicago, Oriental Institute |
| Roman rings | Betrothal rings; Aulus Gellius reported a claim that a nerve ran from the fourth finger of the left hand to the heart. | Aulus Gellius, *Attic Nights* 10.10 |
| Valdaro | Found in 2007 near Mantua: a Neolithic pair about 6,000 years old, aged about 18–20, buried facing each other in an embrace; in the Mantua museum. | *Archaeology* 2008 |
| Modena | Two people buried hand in hand about 1,500 years ago were both men (enamel peptides); their relationship is unknown. | Lugli et al., *Scientific Reports* 2019 |
| Marrying for love | Marriage was mostly about property, alliances and work; love became the ideal in Western Europe and North America from the late 18th century. | Coontz, *Marriage, a History*, 2005 |
| White dress | Queen Victoria married in white silk with Honiton lace on 10 February 1840, chosen to help the Devon lace industry, helping make white fashionable. | Royal Collection Trust |
| Diamond rings | "A Diamond Is Forever" (1947, Frances Gerety at N. W. Ayer for De Beers); named slogan of the century by *Advertising Age* in 1999. | Sotheby's |
| In-laws and alliances | Chapais argues pair bonds created in-laws and peaceful links between groups, and that recognising in-laws is uniquely human (hypothesis). | Chapais, *Primeval Kinship*, 2008 |
| Hxaro | Ju/'hoansi gift-exchange partners: about 16 per person in the early 1970s, some up to 200 km away; about three months a year spent visiting. | Wiessner 1977, 1982 |
