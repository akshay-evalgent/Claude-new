Day One Films' DISCIPLINE is a 2:38 wordless animated short. It reached 5.84 million views in 39 days, on a channel whose typical upload gets 86,000. This page takes it apart, puts numbers on why it spread, shows why the 38 copies of it went nowhere, and proposes the film we should make instead.

The short answer
  1. Why it worked. It took a fight almost everyone has every morning, drew the excuse as a red shadow you can see, put a day counter on screen, and ended on a line people repeat: "Day one, or one day."
  2. Copying it is the one move the data rules out. 38 other channels uploaded a film titled "DISCIPLINE | An Animated Short Film". Together they have 52,134 views, under 1% of the original. The median copy has 72.
  3. Spread is a threshold. Each view has to earn more than one further view (R > 1). Below that line a film stalls near its first audience. Above it, the film runs until it uses up everyone who cares about the theme. So the size of the theme's audience, and a reason to send the film to someone, matter more than polish.
  4. What we should make: LANTERN. A wordless 2:30 film about the parent who got up at 4:30 every day of your childhood. It keeps the proven skeleton and moves to a theme with a far bigger audience, far fewer copies, and a person to send it to.
  5. What we build. An animatic first, then the film plus three vertical cut-downs, made on a reusable animation kit so the second and third films cost much less. Hits are partly luck, so the kit matters as much as the film. The theme radar used for this study is already in the repo.

Part two

Why it spread

Most of what made it work is genre mechanics, which anyone can use. A few things are its own identity, and those we leave alone.

Mechanics we can reuse

  1. A daily fight with near-total reach. Everyone has hit snooze.
  2. The inner voice drawn as a character, its words as objects.
  3. A blank, wordless everyman. Viewers project themselves, and there is no language barrier.
  4. An on-screen counter that turns the story into a progress bar.
  5. Repetition with variation. The alarm returns six times and means something different each time.
  6. Three acts in 158 seconds: fall 38%, climb 40%, payoff 22%.
  7. A new picture about every 2 seconds. Act breaks on black, turns on white flashes.
  8. One accent color with one meaning.
  9. A closing line built to be screenshotted.
  10. An ending that rhymes with the opening.
  11. Packaging: a one-word title in caps, "An Animated Short Film" as a craft signal, the most relatable frame as the thumbnail, chapters written as story beats.

Theirs, so we leave it

  • The white round-headed character and the red shadow design.
  • The 5:00 alarm, the headband, the staircase boss.
  • "Day one, or one day."
  • The word DISCIPLINE in the title.

Where it can be beaten

  • The first two seconds are black.
  • The counter is a label in the corner, outside the world of the film.
  • He is alone. There is nobody in the film you would send it to, so sharing it only says "I'm disciplined".
  • It lives in the male-coded self-improvement niche, which is now flooded.
  • 49 likes per comment: people nod and scroll. A film that invites a personal story can win the comments.
Part three

The math of going viral

Treat every view as something that produces more views. On YouTube most of that comes from the recommendation system: the last people who were shown the film clicked, stayed and seemed satisfied, so it is shown to more people. Shares add the rest. Call the average number of new views each view produces R.

R ≈ CTR × impressions earned per satisfied view + shares per view × views per share
R < 1  total views ≈ seed ÷ (1 − R)
R > 1  share of the audience pool reached, z, solves  z = 1 − e−R·z

The cliff

Near R = 1 a small change in quality makes an enormous change in views. Moving R from 0.90 to 1.10 takes the same 10K seed from 98K views to 3.6M. That is how one channel, making every film the same way, gets results from 18K to 5.8M.

The levers multiply

R is a product: click-through × how long people stay × whether they share. Improve each of four levers by 20% and R rises 1.24 = 2.07×. One weak lever caps all the others, so a great film with a dull thumbnail stays sub-critical.

The ceiling is the pool

Once R > 1 the film reaches a fixed share of everyone who cares about the theme: 58% of the pool at R = 1.5, 80% at R = 2. A theme with three times the audience beats any amount of polish.

The slot is taken

Recommendation is winner-take-most for each interest. The system already has its best "discipline" film to serve, so a copy brings nothing new and its R is near zero.

Shots on goal

Fitting a log-normal curve to those 18 uploads gives about a 7% chance that any one film passes a million views (the raw count is 2 of 18). The chance of at least one hit grows with the number of films made. A well-known experiment (Salganik, Dodds and Watts, 2006, the MusicLab study) found the same pattern: quality raises the odds, but which item wins is partly luck once people influence each other. Plan for a series, not a single film.

Which emotions travel

Berger and Milkman (2012) tracked about 7,000 New York Times articles and the most-emailed list. Content that triggered high-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) was shared more. Sadness lowered sharing. A film that wants to be passed on should end on lift and an action, not on grief.

Part four

Where the open water is

For 20 themes we pulled the top 40 YouTube results for "animated short film" plus the theme. Two numbers per theme: the views of the third-biggest result (proven demand that isn't one fluke) and the share of results under 1,000 views (how crowded the theme is with copies).

Fight films beat feeling films here

On this channel, films about a daily fight with a nameable enemy did best: DISCIPLINE 5.84M, Lust Is Killing You 1.5M, DOPAMINE 570K. Films about an inner state did worst: Loneliness 63K, PERFECTIONISM 33K, The Comparison Trap 18K.

Sequels decay fast

Monk Mode Films' LUST VS LOVE got 2.40M. Part 2 got 318K (13%), Part 4 154K, Part 7 73K. Day One's "Lust Is Killing You." 1.5M was followed by "Lust Is Poison." at 51K. Repeating the promise earns a fraction.

The hit audience didn't stay

88,200 subscribers from about 9.25M channel views, roughly one per 105 views. The most recent uploads sit at 18K to 51K. A hit buys attention once; the next film still has to clear R = 1 on its own.

Part five · the recommendation

Lantern · an animated short film

Every morning at 4:30 a father lights a lantern, scratches one mark on the wall by the door and walks into the dark so his son can sleep. Twenty years later the son, stuck in his own snooze loop in the city, comes home and sees the wall.

Why this theme

  • Bigger pool. Nearly everyone had someone who got up first. It reaches parents as well as their kids, and both sexes.
  • A person to send it to. You send it to your dad, your mum, the family group. A solo film has no recipient; this one does.
  • The right emotion. Gratitude and elevation, ending on an action, not on loss.
  • Open water. Parent and time themes run 15 to 17% copies, against 88% for discipline.
  • Same skeleton. A daily fight, a counter, fall, climb, payoff, a quotable end. The self-improvement audience still gets its film, with a reason behind the grind.

The look

A blue-black night world. The lantern's circle is the only place with warm color and detail, and faces are drawn only when they are lit. Silhouettes keep the acting simple and the style ours.

Lantern amber: duty, love Phone blue: comfort, the son's trap

The counter lives in the world: tally marks on the wall by the door, next to pencil marks of the boy's height. Two counters on one wall, his days and the boy's growth.

Sound

The match strike is the sound logo: first frame, the turn, last frame. A four-note lullaby the father hums in act one becomes the full theme in act three. Wordless choir, no dialogue. The only words on screen are "Dad", "7,300" and the two closing lines, so translation is a caption file.

Vertical cut-downs

The wall (about 30 s): strike, tallies, the reveal, the end line. Two lights (about 20 s): blue against amber. The loop (about 45 s): 4:30 to 4:30, son becomes father. Each ends on its first frame so it replays cleanly.

What to watch after it goes up

  • Click-through against the channel's own average, for each thumbnail in YouTube's Test & Compare.
  • Retention in the first 10 seconds, and whether the curve holds or rises at the 1:24 wall reveal.
  • The R read: if hourly views are still rising 24 to 48 hours after upload, R is above 1. If they are falling, the total will land near first-day views ÷ (1 − R).
  • Comments that mention a parent. That is the share trigger working.

Part seven

What we can build

Build first

The LANTERN film

A 2:30 master in 16:9, three vertical cut-downs and three thumbnails. Animated as code in a Motion artifact you can scrub, comment on by timestamp and export to MP4. Silhouettes, light masks, weather and text suit code well. If the animatic shows a shot that needs real character acting, only that shot goes to a 2D animator.

Build alongside

A reusable film kit

The lantern light mask, tally wall, walk cycles, weather, phone glow, title cards and both aspect ratios, all as parts. Film two and three reuse them. This is what makes 10 to 20 films affordable, and the odds say that is how many it takes.

Done, in the repo

Theme radar

film/viral-study/theme_radar.py reruns the demand-against-crowding scan for any list of themes and scores a channel's uploads against its own median. Run it before choosing each next film.

Small

Pre-flight and launch read

Checks before upload: no black first frame, a picture change at least every 2 seconds, end hold under 1.4 s, cuts on the beat. These reuse the gates from the repo's earlier film test. After upload, the 48-hour R read.

Order of work

  1. Animatic. Rough boards on the beat sheet's timing with temp sound. We judge the story here, before animation costs anything.
  2. Style frames and thumbnails. Three frames that fix the look; the three thumbnail tests.
  3. Animation on the kit, act by act.
  4. Music. The lullaby motif and full theme, original or properly licensed.
  5. Cut-downs from the same scenes.
  6. Pre-flight and publish.
  7. 48-hour read, then pick film two with the radar.

Decisions for you

  • Father or mother first. I'd start with the father: the 4:30 labour image is the clearest. The mother film should be its own film, not "Part 2".
  • Where it lives. A YouTube channel. It doesn't fit the Lean Stack LinkedIn page. A new channel is fine: DISCIPLINE is listed as its channel's oldest upload.
  • Music route. Commission it, or use an AI music tool whose licence covers commercial use.
  • Production route. All code, or code plus a 2D animator for the few acting shots.
Method and limits
  • Video metadata, chapters and the caption track came from YouTube via yt-dlp on 5 November 2026. The beat breakdown uses YouTube's storyboard frames (one every 2 seconds, 160×90). YouTube blocked the full video download, so there is no audio analysis or frame-exact cut timing.
  • Channel-list view counts are rounded by YouTube. Upload dates for the other 17 films were not available (rate limit), so there is no views-per-day comparison between them. "Oldest upload" is inferred from list order.
  • The theme scan is the top 40 results of one search per theme, ranked by relevance. Broad themes surface evergreen studio hits such as Pip and Hair Love; narrow themes surface recent copies. It shows direction, not a census.
  • The spread model's seed (10K) and pool (20M) are illustrative. Concept scores are judgment.
  • Retention curves, click-through and traffic sources are visible only to the channel owner, so they are not in this study.
  • Everything is reproducible from film/viral-study/: viral_math.py prints every number in part three, and theme_radar.py summarize data/search rebuilds part four.
