## What you would see from where you stand (calculated here, all illustrative)

A first-person film shows the surroundings, not you, so these are worked out from the sourced numbers above. None of them comes from a source as stated. Assumptions: you are an ideal radiator of 1.8 square metres (rows 9 and 10), treated as a point; nothing in between absorbs anything (no air, smoke or steam); the ground does not hide anything over the horizon. Real air would absorb much of the ultraviolet and X-rays and turn into a fireball, so the larger distances are a measure of how much energy there is, not a forecast.

"As strong as sunlight" means the same total energy per square metre as the Sun delivers at the Earth's distance (worked out as 1,354 W per square metre from the Sun's 3.828e26 W and 150 million km, both NASA). "Iron melts" means a dark surface facing you would settle at iron's melting point (row 7) if it could only lose heat by glowing.

| Your temperature | Power you give off | As strong as sunlight at | Iron would melt within |
|------------------|--------------------|--------------------------|------------------------|
| 525 °C (row 5) | 41 kilowatts | 1.6 m | 7 cm |
| 1,170 °C (row 6) | 440 kilowatts | 5 m | 24 cm |
| 5,772 K (row 8) | 113 megawatts | 82 m | 3.8 m |
| 30,000 K (close to row 12) | 83 gigawatts | 2.2 km | 100 m |
| 15.7 million K (row 13) | 6.2e21 watts | 605,000 km | 28,500 km |
| 250 million K (row 15) | 3.8e26 watts, the Sun's whole output | 150 million km | 7 million km |

Working: power = 1.8 x 5.67e-8 x T^4. Sunlight distance = square root of (power / (4 x pi x 1,354)). Iron distance = 0.378 m x (T / 1,811)^2.

Past 10,000 K most of this energy is not visible light (row 11), so "as strong as sunlight" is about heat delivered, not about how bright it looks.

## Where popular versions get it wrong

- **"Red hot is the hottest."** Red is the coolest glow. The order is red, orange, yellow, white, blue-white (rows 6 and 11). There is also no "green hot": the Sun peaks in green (about 520 nm) and looks white.
- **"Brighter and bluer forever."** Past about 10,000 K most of the light is ultraviolet, and past about a million K it is X-rays (row 11). To the eye the colour stops changing at blue-white; the extra energy is invisible. A picture that keeps getting bluer and bluer is a drawing choice, not what you would see.
- **"Lightning is hotter than the Sun."** Hotter than the Sun's surface, five times over (row 12). The Sun's core is about 500 times hotter than lightning (15,000,000 / 27,760; my division).
- **"At 15 million degrees you become a star" or "you explode like a hydrogen bomb."** The Sun's core at that temperature makes less heat per cubic metre than a person does (row 14). Fusion there is extremely slow and depends on the crushing density and the Sun's size. Temperature alone does not do it.
- **"5.5 trillion degrees, the hottest thing ever made, measured at CERN."** The measured figure in these sources is about 4 trillion °C at Brookhaven. The 5.5 trillion figure for CERN was an expected value (row 18).
- **"The Planck temperature is the hottest anything can be" ("absolute hot").** It is the temperature beyond which today's physics cannot say what happens. That is not the same as a proven limit (rows 19 and 20).
- **Mixing Celsius and kelvin.** Kelvin is Celsius plus 273. It matters for rows 1 to 8 and stops mattering above about a million degrees. NASA gives the Sun's surface as 5,500 °C and as 5,772 K; both are right.
- **The premise itself.** A real body would not stay a body. It stops being alive at row 3, and long before row 8 it would be gas flying apart. Holding a person-shaped thing together and feeding it endless energy is the one impossible change. Anything drawn as "you" after the first steps is illustrative, and what your surroundings do at each step has no source: the only figures for it are the calculations in "What you would see from where you stand", which rest on stated assumptions.

## Looked for and not confirmed (do not use without checking)

- **Paper catching fire at "451 °F".** A search result said a University of Cambridge safety table gives 218 to 246 °C for paper, but the page refused to load (403), so I did not read it. No verified figure for paper, cloth or wood catching fire.
- **"Tungsten has the highest melting point of any metal."** Widely stated, but the pages that say so (Royal Society of Chemistry, Britannica, Los Alamos, Jefferson Lab) were blocked or moved. Only the melting point itself is confirmed (row 7).
- **How hot the universe was at set moments after the Big Bang** (for example "the last time anything was this hot"). CERN's page gives times but no temperatures; NASA's WMAP page has moved; a Cambridge page was blocked. No verified figures.
- **A direct statement of normal skin area** for row 10. The 1.8 square metres is my assumption.
