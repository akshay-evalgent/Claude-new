# What If You Got Hotter and Couldn't Stop?

A 59.9 s vertical Short with no voice: every word is on screen, in one continuous first-person scene. `script.json` is the source of truth; this is the same thing to read.

**Premise.** A thought experiment, not a forecast: your temperature climbs from 37 °C to the edge of physics in under a minute while you stand in a snowy field at night and watch your own heat light, burn and then bleach out everything around you; the temperatures are real and the body that survives them is pretend.

**Audience.** YouTube Shorts viewers who watch 'what if' science scenarios told in the second person with counters, on a phone, often muted.

**Question.** If you kept getting hotter, how far could it go, and is there a point where it has to stop?

**Share line.** you are literally glowing right now and nobody can see it

**Comment prompt.** The film asks nothing. What viewers will have something to say about: the rule 'Tungsten boils here. Pretend you don't.' and the clock row that reads PRETEND BODY (jokes about the person still standing there); 'Red is the coolest glow there is' (people who assumed red-hot was the top); whether 10^32 K is really a limit (the film says nobody knows); and the last three words, plus the NEXT row that ends on STAY HERE.

**Post caption.** You start at 37 °C. One minute later, physics gives up. Every temperature on the counter is real; the body that survives them is not.

**Planted object.** A snowflake melting on your palm (5.6 s), and again as the last thing seen (56.6 s).

## Post description

Your temperature starts at 37 °C and climbs for one minute without stopping. You stay where you are, in a snowy field at night, and watch what your own heat does to it.

WHAT IS REAL: the temperatures on the counter. Normal body temperature is taken as 37 °C. In heat stroke the body can reach 41 °C or more, and without emergency treatment that can kill. Water boils at 100 °C at sea level, and up to 60% of an adult body is water. Solids begin to give off a dull red glow at roughly 525 °C (an approximate figure). Glowing lava is red at about 600 to 800 °C, orange up to 1,000 °C and yellow up to 1,200 °C: red is the coolest glow there is. Iron melts at 1,538 °C. Tungsten boils at 5,555 °C. The Sun's visible surface is about 5,500 °C. Lightning heats the air to about 28,000 °C. Above roughly 10,000 degrees a glowing object gives off mostly ultraviolet, and above a million mostly X-rays, so to the eye the colour stops at blue-white. The Sun's core is about 15 million °C; that figure comes from models of the Sun, not from a measurement. About 4 trillion °C has been made and measured at Brookhaven's RHIC collider, hot enough that protons and neutrons melt into a soup of quarks and gluons. The Planck temperature is about 1.4 x 10^32 kelvin. And at 37 °C you really do glow, in infrared.

WHAT IS MADE UP, OR NOT KNOWN: you. A real body stops being alive near the second step and would be gas long before the Sun's surface. The film pretends yours holds together and keeps being fed energy; that is the one impossible thing in it. Everything the field does (the red light on the snow, the fence burning, the ground glowing, how far your light reaches) is an illustration built on rough sums that treat you as a perfect radiator of 1.8 square metres with no air in the way. Real air would turn into a fireball. Past the Sun's surface the honest view is only too bright to look at, so the rings, the grain at 4 trillion degrees and the still white at the end are drawings. How fast the counter climbs was chosen to fit a minute. The Planck temperature is not a proven limit: it is where today's physics stops being able to say what happens, and physicists do not agree on whether a highest temperature exists. The snowflake melts because your skin is warm, not because it can see infrared.
