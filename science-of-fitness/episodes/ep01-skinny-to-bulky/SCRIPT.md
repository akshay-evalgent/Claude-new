## How to read the script

Each scene has five parts:
- **Time:** where the scene starts and ends.
- **Visual:** what is on screen, with the file to use. **NEW** marks an asset that still has to be made.
- **Text:** on-screen text, using the template named on the line.
- **Source:** the reference shown bottom-right.
- **Narration:** the voice-over, in quote blocks, ready to record. The narration alone is also in `VO.txt`.

Delivery is calm and clear, like a friend who reads the studies. Pause at each line break.

## Part 1 · The body: why you stay skinny (1:16 to 4:56)

### Scene 5 · 1:16 · Reason 1
**Visual:** Chapter label `REASON 1 · THE MEMORY TRICK`. The rival leans into frame.
**Text:** none

> [RIVAL]: "Bro, I eat like a horse. I just have a fast metabolism."

### Scene 6 · 1:23
**Visual:** A week-view food log in ink. Monday shows a huge biryani plate glowing amber. Tuesday shows a skipped lunch. Wednesday shows a small meal. Then a bar chart for the week appears with a dashed "needed to grow" line. The weekly average sits under the line (**NEW**: food-log diagram).
**Text:** `Big meals are memorable. Small days are not.`

> Here's the first problem. We remember the big meals.
> The wedding buffet. The double burger. The night you finished a whole pizza.
> We forget the skipped breakfast, the lunch that was just tea, and the day we weren't hungry.
> Your body doesn't count the big meal. It counts the whole week.
> And for a lot of skinny guys, the weekly average is just enough to stay the same.

### Scene 7 · 1:56
**Visual:** Zoom ladder template: body, then stomach, then brain, with the brain glowing teal. Use `assets/anatomy/closeups/brain-spine-nerves.png` for the brain step.
**Text:** `Fullness signals: stomach to brain`

> There's a reason for that. When your stomach stretches and food starts arriving, hormones and nerves tell your brain you've had enough.
> Some people get that "full" signal early and strongly.
> So you eat a big lunch, feel stuffed for hours, and your dinner quietly shrinks.
> You're not lying when you say you eat a lot. You're just not eating a lot every day.

### Scene 8 · 2:24 · Reason 2
**Visual:** Chapter label `REASON 2 · YOUR BODY FIGHTS BACK`. An energy-budget bar fills in three blocks: "keeping you alive" (largest), "digesting food" (small), "moving" (the rest).
**Text:** three labels on the bar, with `roughly` in small text
**Source:** a standard physiology textbook breakdown

> Second reason. Your body spends energy in three ways.
> The biggest is just keeping you alive: your heart, brain, organs.
> A small slice goes to digesting food.
> The rest is movement. And most of that isn't the gym. It's walking, standing, fidgeting.

### Scene 9 · 2:43
**Visual:** Back to the 16 silhouettes. The ones who gained little now show glowing teal legs, moving. The `nerve-signals.mp4` loop sits small in a corner.
**Text:** `Eat more → move more, without noticing`
**Source:** Levine et al., Science, 1999

> That's the part that changed the most in the Mayo Clinic study.
> When people ate more, some of them automatically moved more. They didn't decide to. Their nervous system just did it.
> So if you add a little food and the scale doesn't move, it's not because food doesn't work on you.
> Some of it was burned off by movement you didn't notice. You just need a bit more than you think.

### Scene 10 · 3:15 · Reason 3
**Visual:** Chapter label `REASON 3 · NO SIGNAL TO GROW`. Play `assets/anatomy/anim/zoom-to-biceps.mp4`, then cut to `assets/anatomy/micro/muscle-hierarchy.png`.
**Text:** `Muscle = expensive tissue`

> Third reason, and this is the big one.
> Muscle is expensive. It costs energy to build and energy to keep.
> So your body only builds it when it has a reason.

### Scene 11 · 3:30
**Visual:** The muscle-fibre view, `assets/anatomy/micro/mito-in-fibre.png`, with two arrows: a teal "build" arrow and a coral "break down" arrow, on a balance that tips back and forth.
**Text:** `Build vs break down, all day long`

> Inside every muscle fibre, two things are always happening. Your body builds new muscle protein, and it breaks old protein down.
> When you don't train, those stay roughly balanced. Nothing changes.
> Hard training tips the balance toward building, as long as you've eaten enough food and protein.

### Scene 12 · 3:51
**Visual:** The host does a hard push-up set, with the chest glowing coral (**NEW**: host push-up, 2 keyframes driven by pose control). Overlay `assets/anatomy/muscle-highlights/chest-front.png` small.
**Text:** `Hard sets = the signal`

> Hard training means sets that take you close to the point where you can't do another rep.
> That strain on the muscle fibres is the signal.
> No signal, no reason to grow, no matter how much you eat.
> Eat a lot without training, and most of the extra ends up as fat.

### Scene 13 · 4:14 · The ectomorph myth
**Visual:** The rival, arms crossed. A vintage sepia card reading "1940s body-type theory" with three silhouettes gets stamped `MYTH`.
**Text:** none

> [RIVAL]: "Face it, bro. You're an ectomorph. Born skinny."

### Scene 14 · 4:20
**Visual:** A bar chart of 585 small bars, from slightly negative to very tall. Most sit in the middle, and the two extremes glow.
**Text:** `Same 12-week program. Arm size change: −2% to +59%`
**Source:** Hubal et al., Med Sci Sports Exerc, 2005

> "Body types" come from a 1940s psychology theory, not from muscle science.
> Genetics are real, though. In one study, 585 people did the exact same twelve-week arm program.
> Some barely changed. A few grew their arm muscles by more than half.
> Most people landed somewhere in between, and they grew.
> You won't know where you land until you train properly, eat enough and give it time. Most skinny guys never get that far.

### Scene 15 · 4:56

**Text:** none

### Scene 16 · 5:01
**Visual:** Stat-ring template, with two rings filling.
**Text:** `+10–20% calories` and `gain 0.25–0.5% of body weight a week`, plus a callout `60 kg → about 150–300 g a week`
**Source:** Iraki et al., Sports, 2019

> Not quite. Your body can only build muscle so fast.
> Eat way more than that and the rest is stored as fat.
> A review on gaining muscle suggests eating about ten to twenty percent above what you need, and aiming to gain about a quarter to half a percent of your body weight per week.
> For a sixty-kilo guy, that's about one hundred fifty to three hundred grams a week. Slow. Steady.

### Scene 17 · 5:32
**Visual:** A bathroom scale in ink. Seven daily numbers jump around, then a teal line shows the smooth weekly average. A "+300 kcal" chip slides in, shaped like a milk glass plus a banana.
**Text:** `Weigh daily. Trust the weekly average. Flat for 2 weeks? Add ~300 kcal.`

> How do you know if you're eating enough? Use the scale, but use it the right way.
> Weigh yourself every morning, and look at the weekly average, not one day.
> If the average hasn't moved in two weeks, add about three hundred calories a day.
> That's roughly a glass of milk and a banana.

### Scene 18 · 5:56

**Source:** Morton et al., Br J Sports Med, 2018

> Next, protein. It's the raw material.
> A big analysis of forty-nine studies found that gains in muscle levelled off at about one point six grams of protein per kilo of body weight per day.
> For a sixty-kilo guy, that's about ninety-six grams a day.

## Part 3 · The training (7:41 to 10:25)

### Scene 22 · 7:41
**Visual:** Chapter label `PART 3 · THE TRAINING`. Three cards.
**Text:**
- `Hard sets: stop 1–3 reps before you can't do another`
- `About 10+ hard sets per muscle per week`
- `Each muscle 2–3 times a week`

**Source:** Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger, J Sports Sci, 2017; Schoenfeld et al., Sports Med, 2016

> Now the signal. Three rules.
> One. Make your sets hard. Stop one to three reps before you couldn't do another.
> Two. Do about ten or more hard sets per muscle every week.
> Three. Train each muscle two or three times a week, not once.

### Scene 23 · 8:01
**Visual:** Do-and-don't template. The host doing bodyweight push-ups and heavy dumbbell presses side by side, with both chests glowing the same coral.
**Text:** `Light or heavy: both build muscle, if the set is hard`
**Source:** Schoenfeld et al., J Strength Cond Res, 2017

> And good news if you train at home. Lighter loads build muscle too, as long as you take the set close to failure.
> Your muscle can't tell the difference between a dumbbell and your own body weight. It only feels the effort.

### Scene 24 · 8:20 · The six moves
**Visual:** Six quick do-and-don't cards. Each one shows the move, its target muscle glowing coral from the anatomy kit, and an "easier" chip. Every card carries the safety tag.

| Move | Muscle highlight | Form cue (on screen) | Easier version | Harder version |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Push-up | `muscle-highlights/chest-front.png` | Body in one line, elbows about 45° | Hands on a table | Feet on a chair, backpack on |
| Door row | `muscle-highlights/upper-back-back.png` | Pull your chest to the door, squeeze your shoulder blades | Stand more upright | Feet closer to the door |
| Split squat | `muscle-highlights/quads-front.png` | Front knee follows your toes, lower slowly | Hold a wall | Back foot on a chair, backpack |
| Pike push-up | `muscle-highlights/shoulders-front.png` | Hips high, head goes between your hands | Hands on a step | Feet on a chair |
| Single-leg glute bridge | `muscle-highlights/glutes-back.png` | Push through the heel, pause at the top | Two legs | Shoulders on the sofa |
| Towel curl, or chin-ups if you have a bar | `muscle-highlights/biceps-front.png` | Elbows pinned to your sides | Lighter pull | Slower lowering |

> Here are six moves that cover your whole body with no equipment.
> Push-ups for the chest. Keep your body in one straight line and your elbows at about forty-five degrees, not flared out.
> Door rows for the back. Hold both handles of an open door, lean back, and pull your chest to the door.
> Split squats for the legs. Let your front knee follow your toes, and lower yourself slowly.
> Pike push-ups for the shoulders. Hips high, and your head goes down between your hands.
> Single-leg glute bridges. Push through your heel and pause at the top.
> And a towel curl for the biceps, or chin-ups if you have a bar. Keep your elbows pinned.
> Every move has an easier and a harder version, so there's always a version that's hard for you.

### Scene 25 · 9:30
**Visual:** A rep counter. 3 sets × 8–15 reps. When all sets reach 15, a "level up" chip moves to the harder version, and a progress line steps upward.
**Text:** `3 × 8–15. Hit 15 on every set? Make it harder.`

> How to progress: do three sets of eight to fifteen reps.
> When you can do fifteen on every set, switch to the harder version, or add a backpack with books.
> That's it. More reps, then a harder version. Every week, a little more.

### Scene 26 · 9:53
**Visual:** A simple timer ring between sets, counting down from 2:00. A small "warm-up set" chip appears before set one.
**Text:** `Warm up with an easy set. Rest 1–3 minutes between sets.`

> Two small things that make a big difference.
> Start each move with one easy warm-up set.
> And rest one to three minutes between hard sets, so the next set can be hard too. Rushing your rest just makes every set weaker.

### Scene 27 · 10:12
**Visual:** The sleep scene: the host asleep, with a moon. Stat ring `−18%`.
**Text:** `One night without sleep: −18% muscle building`
**Source:** Lamon et al., Physiol Rep, 2021

> And sleep. In one study, a single night without sleep cut muscle building by eighteen percent.
> Seven to nine hours. It's part of the program, not a bonus.

## Quick answers (FAQ) (11:18 to 12:28)

### Scene 30 · 11:18
**Visual:** The rival raises a hand. A teal "Q" card appears: `Won't I just get fat?` Then the scale's weekly-average line climbs slowly in teal, against a steep coral "dirty bulk" line.
**Text:** `Slow gain = mostly muscle. Fast gain = mostly fat.`

> [RIVAL]: "Won't I just get fat?"
> A little fat comes along with any bulk. That's normal.
> Keep the gain slow, a quarter to half a percent of your body weight a week, and most of it goes where you want it.
> If your waist grows much faster than your arms and chest, eat a little less.

### Scene 31 · 11:43
**Visual:** Second Q card: `Do I need supplements?` Shelves of tubs get crossed out until one plain tub remains, labelled "creatine monohydrate", with an amber outline.
**Text:** `Not needed. Creatine monohydrate is the one with strong evidence.`
**Source:** Kreider et al., J Int Soc Sports Nutr, 2017

> "Do I need supplements?"
> No. Food and training do the work.
> If you want one, creatine monohydrate is the most studied. It helps muscle gain during training, and it's considered safe for healthy adults.
> Protein powder is just food in a tub. Useful if it helps you hit your protein, never required.
> Under eighteen, or have a health condition? Ask a doctor first.

### Scene 32 · 12:11
**Visual:** Third Q card: `Can I still do cardio?` The host walks, with legs glowing teal, then plays football.
**Text:** `Walking and sports: yes. Just eat for it.`

> "Can I still do cardio or play sports?"
> Yes. Walking and sports are good for your heart, and they won't stop you growing.
> Just remember they burn calories. On days you play, eat a bit more.
